The previous post ended flat: covariates lost, cross-learning lost, a 46-length context sweep bought 0.11%, a LoRA fine-tune was a wash. One lever was left — treat different items differently: fine-tune per group, cross-learn inside a group, one model per group.

That lever has a precondition, and the precondition is testable before any of it is built. For a static grouping to help, difficulty has to be a stable property of an item. If the items that are hard this week are not the ones that were hard last week, no grouping — hand-made, clustered or learned — has anything to hold on to.

This post answers that, and then keeps narrowing. Difficulty is a stable property of the item. Two thirds of it is volume, which needs no clustering to exploit. The third that survives is real — bigger than every other term in the budget except the irreducible noise — and it is not in the weekly shape, which is the one candidate axis this study had built the apparatus to test.

Three answers, and the last one is the kind of negative result worth publishing: bounded, and it redirects the work rather than ending it.

The panel

Twenty-four origins, one week apart, scored over 7-day non-overlapping windows. Non-overlapping matters: with overlapping windows the correlation between consecutive origins would be partly an artefact of shared days. The model is the zero-shot one at the context length chosen for days 1–7 — the fine-tuned model is disqualified here, because walking its test-origin checkpoint backwards would let it see the future of every earlier window.

The origins run 2015-10-18 to 2016-03-27. Of the 494 series scorable at least once, 138 are scorable at every origin, and those are the panel. That count is printed rather than buried: double-centring a grid with holes is a different estimator, and a series present at half the origins would have its difficulty measured on a different set of weeks than its neighbours’.

The error of each (item, origin) cell splits four ways:

\[x_{i,o} = \mu + \alpha_i + \beta_o + \varepsilon_{i,o}\]

\(\alpha_i\) is the item — by construction the only part a static grouping could exploit. \(\beta_o\) is the week: some weeks are hard for everyone. What is left splits further into a part that survives to the next origin, a part that does not, and the sampling noise of the cell itself, measured from the odd and even days of each window.

Does the estimator work?

This is the step most analyses skip, and the one that decides whether anything here means anything. Before the real panel, a simulated one with known components, at this panel’s size and noise level:

Show the code 
control, planted = simulate_panel(n_series=250, n_origins=N_ORIGINS, seed=SEED)
recovered = decompose(control).shares()
components = ["series", "origin", "persistent", "transient", "noise"]
md(pl.DataFrame({
    "component": components,
    "planted %": [100 * planted[c] for c in components],
    "recovered %": [100 * recovered["series_adjusted" if c == "series" else c]
                    for c in components],
}), digits=1)

Two further controls run silently and both pass: a panel with no item effect returns 0.8% after bias adjustment rather than the 4.5% the raw estimator reports, and a pure-noise panel returns a persistence of +0.005 rather than the negative value double-centring would otherwise induce.

The control also exposes the method’s one real limit. A difficulty that drifts slowly is partly indistinguishable from a constant one over a panel spanning six months, so it gets shared between the item term and the persistent term. Sweeping the drift’s autocorrelation from 0 to 0.97 moves the split between those two from 0.7%/0% to 15.9%/2.0% — while their sum stays put. So two readings get reported: the item share answers could a grouping fitted once and left alone help, and the sum answers is there anything stable at all, even if it has to be refitted.

Two numbers carry the section. The identity of the item accounts for 42% of the variance in log WQL once the noise inside each \(\alpha_i\) is removed. And the week accounts for 2% — there are essentially no weeks that are hard for everybody. Difficulty has an address, and the address is the item.

Most of what remains is sampling noise, which is what forecasting 7-day windows of items selling a few units a week buys you. That matters for the next section rather than being an aside.

Two instruments, one answer

A decomposition is a model, and a model can be wrong. Rank agreement is not a model: order the items by error at one origin, order them again at the next, and ask whether the two orderings agree.

A median of +0.52 between consecutive origins looks like weak agreement. Test–retest conventions call anything under 0.5 weak, and the pre-registered rule for this study wanted 0.6. Taken at face value, the two sections of this post seem to disagree: a large item effect, and a ranking that only half holds.

They do not disagree. The decomposition predicts the rank correlation, and the prediction can be written down from the variance shares alone.

Within one origin the week effect is a constant and does not touch the across-items ranking, so the spread of the measured error at a single origin is \(\operatorname{var}(\alpha) + v_{\text{pers}} + v_{\text{trans}} + v_{\text{noise}}\), while the covariance between two consecutive origins keeps only the parts that survive the step, \(\operatorname{var}(\alpha) + v_{\text{pers}}\). Their ratio is the correlation the model expects anyone to observe. Six months apart the persistent part has decayed and only \(\operatorname{var}(\alpha)\) remains.

Divide instead by the reliable part of a cell — everything except the sampling noise — and you get the correlation between the underlying difficulties rather than between their noisy estimates.

Show the code 
v_alpha = shares["series_adjusted"]
v_pers, v_trans, v_noise = shares["persistent"], shares["transient"], shares["noise"]
cell_variance = v_alpha + v_pers + v_trans + v_noise
reliability = (v_alpha + v_pers + v_trans) / cell_variance

attenuation = pl.DataFrame([
    {"quantity": "consecutive origins",
     "predicted": (v_alpha + v_pers) / cell_variance,
     "observed": ranks["consecutive_median"],
     "noise-corrected": (v_alpha + v_pers) / (v_alpha + v_pers + v_trans)},
    {"quantity": "first vs last origin",
     "predicted": v_alpha / cell_variance,
     "observed": ranks["first_vs_last"],
     "noise-corrected": v_alpha / (v_alpha + v_pers + v_trans)},
])
md(attenuation, digits=3)

A single 7-day window is only 56% reliable: more than two fifths of its variance is the counting noise of an item that sells a handful of units a week. Seen through a ruler that blunt, a difficulty ordering stable at 0.86 between consecutive origins can only show up as +0.52 — which is what it did, to within a few hundredths of the predicted value, on both rows, with the two residuals falling on opposite sides. That is what sampling variation looks like; a systematic error in the variance model would push both the same way.

So the observed +0.52 was never evidence of weak stability. It was evidence of a noisy ruler, and the agreement between the predicted and observed columns is the strongest internal check in this post: a variance model and a distribution-free rank statistic, built on different assumptions, returning the same number.

The six-month row says something further. Nearly all of the long-run agreement is the \(\alpha\) term — the persistent part has decayed by then — so \(\alpha\) is behaving like a genuinely fixed property of the item rather than a slow drift.

The pre-registered rule, and what to do with it

Partial persistence: weigh the gain against the complexity.
  series (bias-adjusted) 41.7% of the variance, median consecutive-origin Spearman +0.52, first vs last +0.41
  stable at all (series + persistent) 45.7%, of which persistent 4.0%

The rule, fixed before any of this was computed: a median consecutive-origin Spearman of 0.6 or more and at least 35% of the variance on the item means a grouping has something real; 0.3 or less, or under 20%, means it has not; between the two, partial. It returns partial, on one arm only — the variance share clears its bar comfortably, the Spearman does not.

Moving a pre-registered threshold after seeing the data is exactly the move this study is built to avoid, so the rule stays as written and its verdict stays reported. But the rule was mis-specified, and identifiably so before any data arrived: its Spearman arm bounds a quantity that the sampling noise of a 7-day window attenuates, and the thresholds were taken from published work on windows of a different reliability. The honest record is both numbers — the rule as written returns partial, and the quantity the rule was reaching for is 0.86.

On the question the rule was asked to settle, the evidence is one-directional:

  • the item carries 42% of the variance, CI [36, 48];
  • the week carries 2%;
  • the underlying difficulty ordering holds at 0.86 week to week and 0.78 across six months;
  • and both known biases of the method push toward a large item effect, so 42% is better read as an upper bound than as a point estimate.

Difficulty is a stable property of the item. The precondition holds.

Most of it is volume

A large item share is the start of an argument, not the end of one. WQL is scale-free, so a dependence on volume would not be an artefact of units — it would be a statement about predictability: an item selling a third of a unit a day has an irreducibly high relative error, and that is a permanent property of the item.

That distinction decides what \(\alpha_i\) is worth. If it is a function of volume, then “group the items by difficulty” means “bucket them by volume”, which needs no clustering and which the forecaster reads off the history unaided.

One correction is mandatory and easy to miss. The sampling noise of a 7-day window is itself strongly volume-dependent, so low-volume items have their \(\alpha\) inflated more — which would manufacture exactly the relationship being tested for. A global bias adjustment cannot fix a bias that differs per item, so each item’s noise is measured from its own odd and even days and removed individually.

Volume and sparsity together explain 63% of the stable difficulty. Two details matter for reading that.

They are not two explanations. Volume alone reaches the same 63%; adding the zero-day share moves nothing. At a Spearman of +0.71 with \(\alpha\) and a near-mechanical relationship to volume on data that is two thirds zeros, sparsity is a second view of the same quantity, not an independent factor.

And the noise channel is not what is driving it. A variance subtraction fixes an inflated spread, not an inflated level, and \(x\) is a log, so a noisier estimate of the same underlying error need not have the same expectation. The direct test is to ask how much of \(\alpha\) the measurement noise itself explains: it reaches 0.63 as a Spearman against 0.77 for volume, and 34% of the variance against 63%. If the volume relationship were an artefact of the noise level, the noise level would be the proximate cause and the stronger correlate. It is the weaker one, by a clear margin.

So two thirds of the stable item effect is a sort on a number already in the history, and one third is not: 15.6% of the whole error budget, against 2.0% for the week and 11.5% for everything transient. It is the largest addressable term left, and the only one a grouping could earn its keep on.

Is the rest of it in the weekly shape?

The residual is real. The question is where to look for it, and this study had already built the machinery to look in one specific place: the weekly shape — the share of a week falling on each weekday. Each week is normalised to 1, the weeks are pooled, and the result is a 7-vector summing to 1, so how much an item sells drops out and only when it sells remains.

Here is that construction, animated, on planted archetypes: four invented weekly shapes, scattered and then redrawn as counts at the items’ real weekly totals. It is synthetic data, and saying so is the point — it shows what the space is and what genuine structure in it would look like, which is what makes the measurement below legible either way.

Shape is worth testing because it is a different axis from volume by construction. But that is also why the link cannot be assumed: the dominant component of the item effect has been removed from the object on purpose, so whether anything of the item effect is left in that object is an open empirical question — and the cheapest one available, because it decides whether a clustering of the shape space is worth building at all.

The test regresses \(\alpha_i\) on volume, sparsity and the per-item noise level, then adds the six free shape coordinates and asks what they add. Three things make it readable:

  • Shape is measured on history strictly before the panel’s first origin. A profile computed over the scored windows would be partly measured on what it predicts.
  • Six extra regressors explain variance by construction — about \(k/n\) of what is left. That is removed in expectation, and a permutation test gives the distribution instead of the expectation. The shuffle is stratified by volume, so the null keeps whatever relationship shape has with volume and destroys only its link to difficulty. Permuting freely would break the shape–volume relationship too, make the null easier to beat, and bias the test toward finding an effect.
  • Shape is itself measured with error, and error in a predictor attenuates what it explains toward zero — the mirror image of the attenuation above. So the increment is reported both as measured and divided by the shape’s own reliability, as an upper bound on what a perfectly measured shape could contribute.

The weekly shape carries essentially none of it. The observed increment is 0.7% of the stable difficulty, below the null’s 95th percentile of 1.5%, at p = 0.15 over 1000 permutations in 10 volume strata. Shape explains less than a typical volume-preserving shuffle does.

And the result is bounded, not merely absent. The profile’s own split-half reliability on these items is 0.44, so a perfectly measured weekly shape could account for at most 1.5% — the fourth bar above. There is no version of this measurement, however much the ruler improves, in which the weekly shape reaches the 15.6% that is still on the item.

Three details that keep it from being an artefact of the setup. 135 of 138 panel items have a 26-week shape before the first origin, so the sub-population is the panel; reliability here (0.44) is better than across the full roster, because the panel’s members are the steady sellers, so this is the favourable corner of the data for shape; and the per-weekday correlations on the right never leave ±0.16, so no single weekday carries difficulty either.

And the audit that was already running

By the time that test existed, the shape space had already been put through a full clustering audit: does the population contain discrete types, or is it one continuum? That audit is worth reporting, with its result stated plainly and its standing corrected.

Inconclusive: the fitted null does not reproduce the real cloud's geometry, so its p-value cannot be read.

Two of its four conditions could not be evaluated, and they fail in opposite directions.

The population was never filtered. The audit was written to keep only the volume deciles whose profile clears a 0.80 reliability bar. No decile cleared it — reliability runs from 0.21 in the sparsest tenth to 0.70 in the densest — so nothing was filtered and the audit ran on all 493 items, including the half whose week-to-week shape variation is indistinguishable from multinomial counting noise. Clustering a cloud in which half the points are near-random draws around the population mean pushes the estimated cluster count toward one, which is the result obtained. The negative finding is the one that defect compromises.

And the null is not usable. It is fitted to the real cloud — dominant axis by SVD, a smooth manifold along it, Gaussian residuals carrying the real residual covariance — but its worst per-coordinate spread comes out 3.9× the real cloud’s, against a refusal threshold of 1.5. A null more diffuse than the data clusters less reproducibly, so the real data beats it too easily and the p-value is biased toward finding structure. So p = 0.024 — which equals its own floor with 40 nulls — cannot be read either.

It would have been easy to publish a continuum here. \(\hat K = 1\) on the real data, every one of the 40 nulls also returning 1, separation 0.00 against a detection floor of 1.50 — three true sentences from which “one continuum, no discrete types” follows smoothly. The checks standing in the way are checks this analysis imposed on itself, and they say the smooth conclusion is unsupported in both directions.

One part of the audit does pass cleanly, and it is a positive structural result: the weekly shapes are anchored to the calendar. The rotation that best aligns a pair of profiles is zero for 37% of pairs against 14% for a randomly rotated copy — a lift of ×2.58. Euclidean distance on these 7-vectors is measuring shape, not clock offset.

What the bridge test does to all of this. Repairing the null and restoring the power were the obvious next steps, and they are no longer worth taking for this purpose: shape carries at most 1.5% of the difficulty that volume leaves unexplained, so even a repaired audit returning crisp discrete types would be organising almost none of the error.

That is not the same as the work being wasted. The audit answers a real question about the data — do items form types in weekly shape — now known to be decoupled from forecast difficulty. And there is a symmetry worth noticing: the audit found shape strongly volume-dependent in its measurability, and the bridge test found it nearly independent of difficulty in its content. Both say the same thing from opposite ends. Shape and difficulty are different axes, and the chain that ran from one to the other was assumed rather than measured.

Where this leaves the question

Established. 42% of the variance in log WQL sits on the identity of the item against 2% on the week, with an underlying week-to-week stability of 0.86 and a six-month stability of 0.78. Of that, 63% is volume — a continuous covariate already present in the history, needing no clustering. The 15.6% that remains is item-specific, stable, and not in the weekly shape, at a ceiling of 1.5%. The shape space is calendar-anchored, and its measurability rises monotonically with volume.

Open. Whether that residual is organised at all, and on what axis. Candidates this study has not tested: burstiness and volatility, lifecycle stage, promotion and price response, intermittency structure beyond the zero-day share. Each is one covariate block through the same machinery — the test is cheap now that the panel and the estimator exist, and a negative is as useful as a positive because it is bounded.

And two limits that no further run removes. The bridge test is linear in the shape coordinates, so a difficulty depending on shape non-monotonically — hard at both ends of the weekend axis — would be missed, and the ceiling is a linear ceiling. And shape here is measured once, well before the scored windows: a profile that drifts could matter nearer them, which is one of the audit steps this study skipped.

Across three posts: a foundation model beat a reproduced top-4% feature pipeline by 8.3% of WMAPE with no features, no tuning and no training, and everything afterwards moved the result by a fraction of a percent. The error that remains is not spread evenly — it sits on particular items, durably, and mostly in proportion to how little they sell. What is left after that is the real target, it is about a seventh of the budget, and the first place this study looked for it is now ruled out. Knowing which axis not to spend a month on is worth more than a clustering that would have organised nothing.

[The previous post](../02-chronos2/) ended flat: covariates lost, cross-learning lost, a
46-length context sweep bought 0.11%, a LoRA fine-tune was a wash. One lever was left —
**treat different items differently**: fine-tune per group, cross-learn inside a group, one
model per group.

That lever has a precondition, and the precondition is testable before any of it is built.
For a static grouping to help, **difficulty has to be a stable property of an item.** If the
items that are hard this week are not the ones that were hard last week, no grouping —
hand-made, clustered or learned — has anything to hold on to.

This post answers that, and then keeps narrowing. Difficulty *is* a stable property of the
item. Two thirds of it is volume, which needs no clustering to exploit. The third that
survives is real — bigger than every other term in the budget except the irreducible noise —
and it is **not** in the weekly shape, which is the one candidate axis this study had built
the apparatus to test.

Three answers, and the last one is the kind of negative result worth publishing: bounded,
and it redirects the work rather than ending it.

## The panel

Twenty-four origins, one week apart, scored over 7-day non-overlapping windows.
Non-overlapping matters: with overlapping windows the correlation between consecutive
origins would be partly an artefact of shared days. The model is the zero-shot one at the
context length chosen for days 1–7 — the fine-tuned model is disqualified here, because
walking its test-origin checkpoint backwards would let it see the future of every earlier
window.

```{python}
#| label: panel-build
#| echo: false
CONTEXT = int(read_json("context_choice")["early_context"])
ORIGINS = origin_grid(VALIDATION_ORIGIN, N_ORIGINS, SEGMENT_DAYS)
sweep = pl.concat([
    pl.read_parquet(experiment_path(f"origin_sweep_ctx{CONTEXT}_origin{origin}", ROSTER))
    for origin in ORIGINS
])
panel = build_panel(cell_wql(sweep, TRUTH, scored_cells(SALES, ORIGINS)))
result = decompose(panel)
shares = result.shares()
n_series, n_origins = panel.shape
```

The origins run `{python} str(ORIGINS[0])` to `{python} str(ORIGINS[-1])`. Of the 494 series
scorable at least once, `{python} str(n_series)` are scorable at **every** origin, and those
are the panel. That count is printed rather than buried: double-centring a grid with holes
is a different estimator, and a series present at half the origins would have its difficulty
measured on a different set of weeks than its neighbours'.


The error of each (item, origin) cell splits four ways:

$$x_{i,o} = \mu + \alpha_i + \beta_o + \varepsilon_{i,o}$$

$\alpha_i$ is the item — by construction the only part a static grouping could exploit.
$\beta_o$ is the week: some weeks are hard for everyone. What is left splits further into a
part that survives to the next origin, a part that does not, and the sampling noise of the
cell itself, measured from the odd and even days of each window.

## Does the estimator work?

This is the step most analyses skip, and the one that decides whether anything here means
anything. Before the real panel, a simulated one with known components, at this panel's size
and noise level:

```{python}
#| label: tbl-control
#| tbl-cap: "Planted components against recovered ones, on a simulated panel of the same shape."
#| code-fold: true
control, planted = simulate_panel(n_series=250, n_origins=N_ORIGINS, seed=SEED)
recovered = decompose(control).shares()
components = ["series", "origin", "persistent", "transient", "noise"]
md(pl.DataFrame({
    "component": components,
    "planted %": [100 * planted[c] for c in components],
    "recovered %": [100 * recovered["series_adjusted" if c == "series" else c]
                    for c in components],
}), digits=1)
```

Two further controls run silently and both pass: a panel with no item effect returns 0.8%
after bias adjustment rather than the 4.5% the raw estimator reports, and a pure-noise panel
returns a persistence of +0.005 rather than the negative value double-centring would
otherwise induce.

The control also exposes the method's one real limit. A difficulty that **drifts slowly** is
partly indistinguishable from a constant one over a panel spanning six months, so it gets
shared between the item term and the persistent term. Sweeping the drift's autocorrelation
from 0 to 0.97 moves the split between those two from 0.7%/0% to 15.9%/2.0% — while their
**sum** stays put. So two readings get reported: the item share answers *could a grouping
fitted once and left alone help*, and the sum answers *is there anything stable at all, even
if it has to be refitted*.

## Where the error lives

```{python}
#| label: tbl-budget
#| tbl-cap: "The variance budget of log WQL. Intervals from 1,000 resamples of the items — not of the cells, since cells within an item are not independent and that dependence is the thing being measured."
#| code-fold: true
intervals = bootstrap_shares(panel, n_draws=1000, seed=SEED)
md(pl.DataFrame([
    {"component": name, "share %": 100 * shares[key],
     "CI low %": 100 * intervals[key][0], "CI high %": 100 * intervals[key][1]}
    for name, key in [("item (raw)", "series"),
                      ("item (bias-adjusted)", "series_adjusted"),
                      ("week", "origin"),
                      ("item x week", "interaction"),
                      ("  - persistent", "persistent"),
                      ("  - transient", "transient"),
                      ("  - sampling noise", "noise"),
                      ("stable (item + persistent)", "stable")]]), digits=1)
```

Two numbers carry the section. **The identity of the item accounts for
`{python} f"{100 * shares['series_adjusted']:.0f}"`% of the variance in log WQL** once the
noise inside each $\alpha_i$ is removed. And **the week accounts for
`{python} f"{100 * shares['origin']:.0f}"`%** — there are essentially no weeks that are hard
for everybody. Difficulty has an address, and the address is the item.

Most of what remains is sampling noise, which is what forecasting 7-day windows of items
selling a few units a week buys you. That matters for the next section rather than being an
aside.

## Two instruments, one answer

A decomposition is a model, and a model can be wrong. Rank agreement is not a model: order
the items by error at one origin, order them again at the next, and ask whether the two
orderings agree.

```{python}
#| label: fig-ranks
#| fig-cap: "Left: Spearman correlation of the difficulty ranking between consecutive origins. Right: first origin against last, six months apart."
#| code-fold: true
ranks = rank_agreement(panel, decile=0.10)
fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(11, 3.6))
ax = axes[0]
values = ranks["consecutive"]
ax.plot(range(1, len(values) + 1), values, color=plots.MAIN_COLOR, marker="o", markersize=4)
ax.axhline(ranks["consecutive_median"], color=plots.TEXT_COLOR, linestyle="--", linewidth=1)
ax.axhspan(-0.5, 0.5, color=plots.GRID_COLOR, alpha=0.6, zorder=0)
ax.text(0.98, 0.04, "|rho| < 0.5: 'weak' for test-retest", transform=ax.transAxes,
        ha="right", va="bottom", color=plots.MUTED_TEXT_COLOR, fontsize=8)
ax.set_ylim(-1, 1)
ax.set_xlabel("pair of consecutive origins")
ax.set_ylabel("Spearman of the difficulty ranking")
ax = axes[1]
first, last = panel.x[:, 0], panel.x[:, -1]
ax.scatter(first, last, s=10, color=plots.MAIN_COLOR, alpha=0.6)
limits = [min(first.min(), last.min()), max(first.max(), last.max())]
ax.plot(limits, limits, color=plots.MUTED_TEXT_COLOR, linewidth=1)
ax.set_xlabel(f"log WQL at {panel.origins[0]}")
ax.set_ylabel(f"... at {panel.origins[-1]}")
ax.text(0.03, 0.95, f"Spearman {ranks['first_vs_last']:+.2f}", transform=ax.transAxes,
        va="top", color=plots.TEXT_COLOR)
fig.tight_layout()
plt.show()
```

A median of `{python} f"{ranks['consecutive_median']:+.2f}"` between consecutive origins
looks like weak agreement. Test–retest conventions call anything under 0.5 weak, and the
pre-registered rule for this study wanted 0.6. Taken at face value, the two sections of this
post seem to disagree: a large item effect, and a ranking that only half holds.

They do not disagree. **The decomposition predicts the rank correlation**, and the
prediction can be written down from the variance shares alone.

Within one origin the week effect is a constant and does not touch the across-items ranking,
so the spread of the measured error at a single origin is
$\operatorname{var}(\alpha) + v_{\text{pers}} + v_{\text{trans}} + v_{\text{noise}}$, while
the covariance between two consecutive origins keeps only the parts that survive the step,
$\operatorname{var}(\alpha) + v_{\text{pers}}$. Their ratio is the correlation the model
expects anyone to *observe*. Six months apart the persistent part has decayed and only
$\operatorname{var}(\alpha)$ remains.

Divide instead by the **reliable** part of a cell — everything except the sampling noise —
and you get the correlation between the underlying difficulties rather than between their
noisy estimates.

```{python}
#| label: tbl-attenuation
#| tbl-cap: "The decomposition's prediction, the rank statistic's observation, and the correlation between the underlying difficulties."
#| code-fold: true
v_alpha = shares["series_adjusted"]
v_pers, v_trans, v_noise = shares["persistent"], shares["transient"], shares["noise"]
cell_variance = v_alpha + v_pers + v_trans + v_noise
reliability = (v_alpha + v_pers + v_trans) / cell_variance

attenuation = pl.DataFrame([
    {"quantity": "consecutive origins",
     "predicted": (v_alpha + v_pers) / cell_variance,
     "observed": ranks["consecutive_median"],
     "noise-corrected": (v_alpha + v_pers) / (v_alpha + v_pers + v_trans)},
    {"quantity": "first vs last origin",
     "predicted": v_alpha / cell_variance,
     "observed": ranks["first_vs_last"],
     "noise-corrected": v_alpha / (v_alpha + v_pers + v_trans)},
])
md(attenuation, digits=3)
```

A single 7-day window is only `{python} f"{reliability:.0%}"` reliable: more than two fifths
of its variance is the counting noise of an item that sells a handful of units a week. Seen
through a ruler that blunt, **a difficulty ordering stable at
`{python} f"{attenuation['noise-corrected'][0]:.2f}"` between consecutive origins can only
show up as `{python} f"{attenuation['observed'][0]:+.2f}"`** — which is what it did, to
within a few hundredths of the predicted value, on both rows, with the two residuals falling
on opposite sides. That is what sampling variation looks like; a systematic error in the
variance model would push both the same way.

So the observed `{python} f"{ranks['consecutive_median']:+.2f}"` was never evidence of weak
stability. It was evidence of a noisy ruler, and the agreement between the predicted and
observed columns is the strongest internal check in this post: a variance model and a
distribution-free rank statistic, built on different assumptions, returning the same number.

The six-month row says something further. Nearly all of the long-run agreement is the
$\alpha$ term — the persistent part has decayed by then — so $\alpha$ is behaving like a
genuinely fixed property of the item rather than a slow drift.

### The pre-registered rule, and what to do with it

```{python}
#| label: verdict-09
#| echo: false
#| output: asis
print("```\n" + verdict(result, ranks, intervals) + "\n```")
```

The rule, fixed before any of this was computed: a median consecutive-origin Spearman of
**0.6 or more and at least 35%** of the variance on the item means a grouping has something
real; **0.3 or less, or under 20%**, means it has not; between the two, partial. It returns
*partial*, on one arm only — the variance share clears its bar comfortably, the Spearman
does not.

Moving a pre-registered threshold after seeing the data is exactly the move this study is
built to avoid, so the rule stays as written and its verdict stays reported. But the rule was
**mis-specified, and identifiably so before any data arrived**: its Spearman arm bounds a
quantity that the sampling noise of a 7-day window attenuates, and the thresholds were taken
from published work on windows of a different reliability. The honest record is both numbers
— the rule as written returns partial, and the quantity the rule was reaching for is
`{python} f"{attenuation['noise-corrected'][0]:.2f}"`.

On the question the rule was asked to settle, the evidence is one-directional:

- the item carries `{python} f"{100 * shares['series_adjusted']:.0f}"`% of the variance, CI
  [`{python} f"{100 * intervals['series_adjusted'][0]:.0f}"`,
  `{python} f"{100 * intervals['series_adjusted'][1]:.0f}"`];
- the week carries `{python} f"{100 * shares['origin']:.0f}"`%;
- the underlying difficulty ordering holds at
  `{python} f"{attenuation['noise-corrected'][0]:.2f}"` week to week and
  `{python} f"{attenuation['noise-corrected'][1]:.2f}"` across six months;
- and both known biases of the method push *toward* a large item effect, so
  `{python} f"{100 * shares['series_adjusted']:.0f}"`% is better read as an upper bound than
  as a point estimate.

**Difficulty is a stable property of the item.** The precondition holds.

## Most of it is volume

A large item share is the start of an argument, not the end of one. WQL is scale-free, so a
dependence on volume would not be an artefact of units — it would be a statement about
predictability: an item selling a third of a unit a day has an irreducibly high *relative*
error, and that is a permanent property of the item.

That distinction decides what $\alpha_i$ is worth. If it is a function of volume, then
"group the items by difficulty" means "bucket them by volume", which needs no clustering and
which the forecaster reads off the history unaided.

One correction is mandatory and easy to miss. The sampling noise of a 7-day window is itself
strongly volume-dependent, so low-volume items have their $\alpha$ inflated **more** — which
would manufacture exactly the relationship being tested for. A global bias adjustment cannot
fix a bias that differs per item, so each item's noise is measured from its own odd and even
days and removed individually.

```{python}
#| label: tbl-volume
#| tbl-cap: "What the stable item effect is made of. Each row removes one more thing; the share is of the whole error budget."
#| code-fold: true
volume_record = RECORD["stable_vs_volume"]
rho = volume_record["spearman"]
md(pl.DataFrame([
    {"removed": "nothing (raw item effect)", "Spearman with alpha": None,
     "item share of the budget %": 100 * volume_record["share_raw"]},
    {"removed": "each item's own measured noise", "Spearman with alpha": None,
     "item share of the budget %": 100 * volume_record["share_noise_corrected"]},
    {"removed": "+ log volume", "Spearman with alpha": rho["log volume"],
     "item share of the budget %": None},
    {"removed": "+ zero-day share", "Spearman with alpha": rho["zero-day share"],
     "item share of the budget %": None},
    {"removed": "+ both covariates", "Spearman with alpha": None,
     "item share of the budget %": 100 * volume_record["share_after_covariates"]},
]), digits=2)
```

```{python}

                     (axes[1], aligned["zero_share"].to_numpy(), "share of zero-sale days")]:

               color=plots.MAIN_COLOR, alpha=0.55, edgecolor="none")
    ax.set_xlabel(label)
    ax.set_ylabel(r"$\alpha_i$  (stable difficulty)")
axes[0].text(0.03, 0.06, f"Spearman {rho['log volume']:+.2f}", transform=axes[0].transAxes,
             color=plots.TEXT_COLOR)
axes[1].text(0.03, 0.94, f"Spearman {rho['zero-day share']:+.2f}",

```

Volume and sparsity together explain
**`{python} f"{100 * volume_record['r2_of_alpha']:.0f}"`%** of the stable difficulty. Two
details matter for reading that.

**They are not two explanations.** Volume alone reaches the same
`{python} f"{100 * volume_record['r2_of_alpha']:.0f}"`%; adding the zero-day share moves
nothing. At a Spearman of `{python} f"{rho['zero-day share']:+.2f}"` with $\alpha$ and a
near-mechanical relationship to volume on data that is two thirds zeros, sparsity is a second
view of the same quantity, not an independent factor.

**And the noise channel is not what is driving it.** A variance subtraction fixes an inflated
spread, not an inflated *level*, and $x$ is a log, so a noisier estimate of the same
underlying error need not have the same expectation. The direct test is to ask how much of
$\alpha$ the measurement noise itself explains: it reaches
`{python} f"{abs(rho['noise level (control)']):.2f}"` as a Spearman against
`{python} f"{abs(rho['log volume']):.2f}"` for volume, and 34% of the variance against 63%.
If the volume relationship were an artefact of the noise level, the noise level would be the
proximate cause and the stronger correlate. It is the weaker one, by a clear margin.

So **two thirds of the stable item effect is a sort on a number already in the history**, and
one third is not:
`{python} f"{100 * volume_record['share_after_covariates']:.1f}"`% of the whole error budget,
against `{python} f"{100 * shares['origin']:.1f}"`% for the week and
`{python} f"{100 * (shares['persistent'] + shares['transient']):.1f}"`% for everything
transient. It is the largest addressable term left, and the only one a grouping could earn
its keep on.

## Is the rest of it in the weekly shape?

The residual is real. The question is where to look for it, and this study had already built
the machinery to look in one specific place: the **weekly shape** — the share of a week
falling on each weekday. Each week is normalised to 1, the weeks are pooled, and the result
is a 7-vector summing to 1, so how much an item sells drops out and only *when* it sells
remains.


Here is that construction, animated, on **planted archetypes**: four invented weekly shapes,
scattered and then redrawn as counts at the items' real weekly totals. It is synthetic data,
and saying so is the point — it shows what the space is and what genuine structure in it
would look like, which is what makes the measurement below legible either way.

Shape is worth testing because it is a different axis from volume by construction. But that
is also why the link cannot be assumed: the dominant component of the item effect has been
removed from the object on purpose, so whether anything of the item effect is left *in* that
object is an open empirical question — and the cheapest one available, because it decides
whether a clustering of the shape space is worth building at all.

The test regresses $\alpha_i$ on volume, sparsity **and the per-item noise level**, then adds
the six free shape coordinates and asks what they add. Three things make it readable:

- **Shape is measured on history strictly before the panel's first origin.** A profile
  computed over the scored windows would be partly measured on what it predicts.
- **Six extra regressors explain variance by construction** — about $k/n$ of what is left.
  That is removed in expectation, and a **permutation test** gives the distribution instead
  of the expectation. The shuffle is **stratified by volume**, so the null keeps whatever
  relationship shape has with volume and destroys only its link to difficulty. Permuting
  freely would break the shape–volume relationship too, make the null easier to beat, and
  bias the test toward finding an effect.
- **Shape is itself measured with error**, and error in a *predictor* attenuates what it
  explains toward zero — the mirror image of the attenuation above. So the increment is
  reported both as measured and divided by the shape's own reliability, as an upper bound on
  what a perfectly measured shape could contribute.

```{python}
#| label: fig-shape-test
#| fig-cap: "Left: what the weekly shape adds to the stable difficulty, against the volume-stratified permutation null. Right: each weekday's own rank correlation with difficulty, before volume is removed."
```

**The weekly shape carries essentially none of it.** The observed increment is
`{python} f"{100 * bridge['increment']:.1f}"`% of the stable difficulty, *below* the null's
95th percentile of `{python} f"{100 * bridge['null_p95']:.1f}"`%, at
p = `{python} f"{bridge['p_value']:.2f}"` over
`{python} str(bridge['n_permutations'])` permutations in
`{python} str(bridge['n_strata'])` volume strata. Shape explains less than a typical
volume-preserving shuffle does.

And the result is **bounded**, not merely absent. The profile's own split-half reliability on
these items is `{python} f"{bridge['shape_reliability']:.2f}"`, so a perfectly measured
weekly shape could account for at most
`{python} f"{100 * bridge['increment_disattenuated']:.1f}"`% — the fourth bar above. There is
no version of this measurement, however much the ruler improves, in which the weekly shape
reaches the `{python} f"{100 * volume_record['share_after_covariates']:.1f}"`% that is still
on the item.

Three details that keep it from being an artefact of the setup.
`{python} str(bridge['n_series_with_shape'])` of `{python} str(bridge['n_series_panel'])`
panel items have a 26-week shape before the first origin, so the sub-population is the panel;
reliability here (`{python} f"{bridge['shape_reliability']:.2f}"`) is *better* than across the
full roster, because the panel's members are the steady sellers, so this is the favourable
corner of the data for shape; and the per-weekday correlations on the right never leave
±0.16, so no single weekday carries difficulty either.

## And the audit that was already running

By the time that test existed, the shape space had already been put through a full clustering
audit: does the population contain **discrete types**, or is it one continuum? That audit is
worth reporting, with its result stated plainly and its standing corrected.

Two of its four conditions could not be evaluated, and they fail in opposite directions.

**The population was never filtered.** The audit was written to keep only the volume deciles
whose profile clears a 0.80 reliability bar. No decile cleared it — reliability runs from 0.21
in the sparsest tenth to 0.70 in the densest — so nothing was filtered and the audit ran on
all `{python} str(AUDIT['n_series_audited'])` items, including the half whose week-to-week
shape variation is indistinguishable from multinomial counting noise. Clustering a cloud in
which half the points are near-random draws around the population mean **pushes the estimated
cluster count toward one**, which is the result obtained. The negative finding is the one that
defect compromises.

**And the null is not usable.** It is fitted to the real cloud — dominant axis by SVD, a
smooth manifold along it, Gaussian residuals carrying the real residual covariance — but its
worst per-coordinate spread comes out
`{python} f"{AUDIT['null_geometry_worst_spread_ratio']:.1f}"`× the real cloud's, against a
refusal threshold of 1.5. A null more diffuse than the data clusters less reproducibly, so
the real data beats it too easily and the p-value is biased **toward** finding structure. So
p = `{python} f"{AUDIT['p_value']:.3f}"` — which equals its own floor with
`{python} str(AUDIT['n_nulls'])` nulls — cannot be read either.

It would have been easy to publish a continuum here. $\hat K = 1$ on the real data, every one
of the `{python} str(AUDIT['n_nulls'])` nulls also returning 1, separation
`{python} f"{AUDIT['separation_achieved']:.2f}"` against a detection floor of
`{python} f"{AUDIT['detection_floor_delta']:.2f}"` — three true sentences from which "one
continuum, no discrete types" follows smoothly. The checks standing in the way are checks
this analysis imposed on itself, and they say the smooth conclusion is unsupported in both
directions.

One part of the audit does pass cleanly, and it is a positive structural result: the weekly
shapes are **anchored to the calendar**. The rotation that best aligns a pair of profiles is
zero for `{python} f"{100 * AUDIT['alignment']['share_tau_zero']:.0f}"`% of pairs against
`{python} f"{100 * AUDIT['alignment']['share_tau_zero_reference']:.0f}"`% for a randomly
rotated copy — a lift of ×`{python} f"{AUDIT['alignment']['lift']:.2f}"`. Euclidean distance
on these 7-vectors is measuring shape, not clock offset.

**What the bridge test does to all of this.** Repairing the null and restoring the power were
the obvious next steps, and they are no longer worth taking *for this purpose*: shape carries
at most `{python} f"{100 * bridge['increment_disattenuated']:.1f}"`% of the difficulty that
volume leaves unexplained, so even a repaired audit returning crisp discrete types would be
organising almost none of the error.

That is not the same as the work being wasted. The audit answers a real question about the
data — do items form types in weekly shape — now known to be **decoupled from forecast
difficulty**. And there is a symmetry worth noticing: the audit found shape strongly
volume-dependent in its *measurability*, and the bridge test found it nearly independent of
difficulty in its *content*. Both say the same thing from opposite ends. Shape and difficulty
are different axes, and the chain that ran from one to the other was assumed rather than
measured.

## Where this leaves the question

**Established.**
`{python} f"{100 * shares['series_adjusted']:.0f}"`% of the variance in log WQL sits on the
identity of the item against `{python} f"{100 * shares['origin']:.0f}"`% on the week, with an
underlying week-to-week stability of
`{python} f"{attenuation['noise-corrected'][0]:.2f}"` and a six-month stability of
`{python} f"{attenuation['noise-corrected'][1]:.2f}"`. Of that,
`{python} f"{100 * volume_record['r2_of_alpha']:.0f}"`% is volume — a continuous covariate
already present in the history, needing no clustering. The
`{python} f"{100 * volume_record['share_after_covariates']:.1f}"`% that remains is
item-specific, stable, and **not** in the weekly shape, at a ceiling of
`{python} f"{100 * bridge['increment_disattenuated']:.1f}"`%. The shape space is
calendar-anchored, and its measurability rises monotonically with volume.

**Open.** Whether that residual is organised at all, and on what axis. Candidates this study
has not tested: burstiness and volatility, lifecycle stage, promotion and price response,
intermittency structure beyond the zero-day share. Each is one covariate block through the
same machinery — the test is cheap now that the panel and the estimator exist, and a negative
is as useful as a positive because it is bounded.

**And two limits that no further run removes.** The bridge test is **linear** in the shape
coordinates, so a difficulty depending on shape non-monotonically — hard at both ends of the
weekend axis — would be missed, and the ceiling is a linear ceiling. And shape here is
measured once, well before the scored windows: a profile that *drifts* could matter nearer
them, which is one of the audit steps this study skipped.
