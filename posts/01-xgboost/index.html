A foundation model is only impressive against something. So I began by building the reference it would have to beat: a feature-engineered gradient-boosting model, in the tradition that won M5, inspired by a public top-4% solution.

The result worth stating up front is how little those 52 engineered features buy over the mean of the last 28 days. That is not a criticism of the solution I reproduced — I reduced it to a single XGBoost model where the original used LightGBM — but it is what makes the comparison in the next post far more interesting.

The dataset

One target value per series per day: units of one item sold in one Walmart store. History runs 2011-01-29 to 2016-05-22, 1,941 days, 30,490 series. I work throughout on a frozen random sample of 500 series, drawn once and saved, so every number in these three posts comes from the same items. The sample is for tractability, nothing more.

The median series sells 0.55 units a day and is silent on 66% of the days it is on the shelf. Two consequences run through everything that follows.

First, a zero is usually information, not a gap. The one exception is structural: days before an item’s first price in sell_prices.csv, when it was not on the shelf at all. Those are dropped from every history. Everything else is a real zero — the item was available and nobody bought it — and it is part of what the forecast has to get right.

Second, the evaluation metric over a period of zero sales cannot be a percentage error. Dividing by an actual of zero is undefined, and most actuals are zero.

The harness comes first

I set two forecast origins:

A (series, window) pair is scored only if it clears three conditions: at least 56 days (arbitrary, but reasonable) on sale before the origin, no missing day inside the window, and at least one unit sold in the window. The third is necessary — WMAPE and WQL divide by the window’s total, so a series that sells nothing has no defined error.

What matters more than the conditions is that I report the count each one removes separately, and that the surviving list is frozen to disk. A roster that quietly changes between models turns every later comparison into a comparison of populations.

Of the 500 sampled series, 473 are scorable on the validation window and 485 on the test window. In both cases every exclusion is the no-sale condition: none of the 500 is short of history, and none has a missing day inside either window.

Five metrics, and what each is for

metric definition what it is for
MAE mean of \(\lvert y - \hat y\rvert\) error in units sold
WMAPE \(100 \cdot \sum \lvert y - \hat y\rvert / \sum y\) scale-free, survives zeros: the headline
P90 90th percentile of \(\lvert y - \hat y\rvert\) the bad days
WQL mean over \(q \in \{0.1,\dots,0.9\}\) of \(2 \sum L_q / \sum y\) the whole forecast distribution
RQL \(100 \cdot \sum L_{0.2}(y, \hat y) / \sum y\) the point forecast, with over-prediction priced at four times under-prediction

\(L_q\) is the quantile loss on a forecast \(f\): \(q\,(y - f)\) when \(f\) is too low, \((1-q)(f - y)\) when it is too high. WQL applies it to each quantile forecast \(\hat y_q\) in turn, which is where it is a proper scoring rule — minimised by reporting the true quantile. RQL applies \(L_{0.2}\) to the point forecast \(\hat y\) instead, because the asymmetry is meant to price the cost of acting on the number the model puts forward. Scoring it on the 0.2 quantile column would reward a model for submitting a deliberately low number: on this data that is worth a factor of about 2.8 at identical forecasting skill. All sums run over scored (series, day) pairs, never averaged per series and then averaged again.

One identity decides how the next post must be read: for a model that only gives a point forecast, every quantile column is a copy of it, and WQL collapses to WMAPE/100. A probabilistic model can beat that number only by saying something true about its own uncertainty. So WQL is not a fair axis for comparing a point model with a distributional one; MAE, WMAPE, P90 and RQL are.

I check the metric code against three cases whose answers are known in advance: a perfect forecast scores 0 everywhere; a constant zero forecast scores WMAPE 100, WQL 1 and RQL 20; and a point forecast’s WQL equals its WMAPE/100.

Three naive baselines

Always zero, repeat-last-week, and the mean of the last 28 days. On intermittent data the first is not absurd, and the third is a genuinely strong competitor — which is exactly why it is here.

Show the code 
baselines = ["zero", "seasonal_naive", "moving_average_28d"]

   .select(["model", "n_series"] + metrics.METRICS).sort("WMAPE"))

Note the ordering: repeating last week is barely better than predicting nothing at all, while a flat 28-day mean removes about a fifth of the error. On series this sparse the weekly pattern of a single item is mostly noise, and averaging it away beats reproducing it.

RQL inverts the ranking — the zero forecast wins it outright, 20 against 39. That is the metric doing its job, showing how low volume the real values mostly are.

The features

Rather than invent a feature set, I reproduced one that is public and placed well: btrotta/kaggle-m5 (predict.py), a top-4% M5 solution. Fifty-two features in four families:

  • the series’ own past — lags 1 to 7, means of the last 7/14/28 days, running mean and variance overall and per weekday;
  • the same, two levels up — the item’s department in its store, and the item across all stores, so a thin series borrows the shape of its neighbours;
  • calendar — weekday, month, SNAP benefit days, and an “event uplift” measured as mean sales n days after an event minus the same weekday a week later;
  • price — the current price and its change over 7 and 14 days, plus days since first sale.

The strategy is direct: 28 models, one per forecast day. The model for day k sees sales only up to the origin, with its lag features shifted k days further back, so no forecast is ever fed back as an input and nothing in the window leaks.

Five differences from the original are deliberate:

  • XGBoost instead of LightGBM, with the closest equivalent settings;
  • training on the 500-series sample rather than all 30,490 — though the department and item features are still computed on all of them;
  • boosting rounds chosen by early stopping on the last 28 days before the origin, where the original stops on the window it then reports;
  • the event uplift implemented as intended rather than as coded;
  • missing days defined by the price file.

The second and third cost real accuracy, and I kept them anyway: a reproduction that scored better than the original would be the suspicious outcome.

Fifty-two features, 28 trained models and roughly 84 minutes of CPU at the test origin, against a flat mean of the last 28 days: 0.8 WMAPE points on validation, 1.2 on test. The horizon view says where that comes from — the two are nearly tied over the second week, then the mean decays while the model does not, which is the one thing a model with a calendar in it ought to manage. The first week is to the model’s advantage too.

The per-series view is blunter still.

Show the code 
def per_series_wql(model):
    return metrics.score(scored(model, "validation"), by="id").select(
        "id", pl.col("WQL").alias(f"WQL_{model}"))

volume = (scored("xgboost_btrotta", "validation")
          .group_by("id").agg(pl.col("y").sum().alias("volume")))
comparison = (per_series_wql("moving_average_28d")
              .join(per_series_wql("xgboost_btrotta"), on="id")
              .join(volume, on="id"))

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(11, 3.5))
share = plots.index_plot(ax, comparison, "WQL",
                         "moving_average_28d", "xgboost_btrotta", order_by="volume")
plt.show()

XGBoost has the lower WQL on 43% of series. It loses on the majority of items and still wins overall, because the items it wins on are the ones that sell. Pooled metrics weight by volume; a per-series count does not. Both numbers are true, and quoting only the pooled one is exactly the kind of claim I built this harness to catch.

What the baseline settles

Three things carry into the next post.

A number to beat. WMAPE 78.8 on validation, 76.4 on test — and the honest framing is that most of that was already available from a 28-day mean.

An axis on which to beat it. XGBoost returns a point forecast, so the comparison is MAE, WMAPE and P90. Its WQL is arithmetic, not a measurement.

A caveat that cuts the other way. XGBoost is trained on squared error, so it estimates a conditional mean. On absolute-error metrics the optimal point forecast is the median, so any model that returns a median starts with a structural advantage on MAE and WMAPE that has nothing to do with forecasting better. Chronos-2 returns a median, so in case of a tight match strong conclusions could be risky.
