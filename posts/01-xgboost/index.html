Before comparing a foundation model to anything, there has to be something to compare it to, and a way of comparing that cannot quietly flatter either side. This post builds both: the measuring harness, and a feature-engineered gradient-boosting model reproduced from a public top-4% M5 solution.

The result worth stating up front is how little the 52 engineered features buy over a 28-day mean. That is not a criticism of the solution — it is what makes the comparison in the next post informative.

What a zero means

One target value per series per day: units of one item sold in one Walmart store. History runs 2011-01-29 to 2016-05-22, 1,941 days, 30,490 series. All the work here is on a frozen random sample of 500, drawn once and saved, so every notebook studies the same items.

on_sale = SALES.filter(~pl.col("is_missing"))

median_daily = per_series["mean_daily"].median()

The median series sells 0.55 units a day and is silent on 66% of the days it is on the shelf. Two consequences run through everything that follows.

First, a zero is usually information, not a gap. The one exception is structural: days before an item’s first price in sell_prices.csv, when it was not on the shelf at all. Those are dropped from every history. Everything else is a real zero — the item was available and nobody bought it — and it is part of what the forecast has to get right.

Second, the metric cannot be a percentage error. Dividing by an actual of zero is undefined, and most actuals are zero.

The harness comes first

Two forecast origins, fixed before any model ran:

A (series, window) pair is scored only if it clears three conditions: at least 56 days on sale before the origin, no missing day inside the window, and at least one unit sold in the window. The third is not cosmetic — WMAPE and WQL divide by the window’s total, so a series that sells nothing has no defined error.

What matters more than the conditions is that the count removed by each one is reported separately, and that the surviving list is frozen to disk. A roster that silently changes between models turns every later comparison into a comparison of populations.

Show the code

Of the 500 sampled series, 473 are scorable on the validation window and 485 on the test window. In both cases every exclusion is the no-sale condition: none of the 500 is short of history, and none has a missing day inside either window.

Five metrics, and what each is for

metric definition what it is for
MAE mean of \(\lvert y - \hat y\rvert\) error in units sold
WMAPE \(100 \cdot \sum \lvert y - \hat y\rvert / \sum y\) scale-free, survives zeros: the headline
P90 90th percentile of \(\lvert y - \hat y\rvert\) the bad days
WQL mean over \(q \in \{0.1,\dots,0.9\}\) of \(2 \sum L_q / \sum y\) the whole forecast distribution
RQL \(100 \cdot \sum L_{0.2} / \sum y\) over-prediction priced at four times under-prediction

\(L_q\) is the quantile loss: \(q\,(y - \hat y_q)\) when the forecast is too low, \((1-q)(\hat y_q - y)\) when it is too high. All sums run over scored (series, day) pairs, never averaged per series and then averaged again.

One identity decides how the next post must be read: for a model that only gives a point forecast, every quantile column is a copy of it, and WQL collapses to WMAPE/100. A probabilistic model can beat that number only by saying something true about its own uncertainty. So WQL is not a fair axis for comparing a point model with a distributional one; MAE, WMAPE and P90 are.

The metric code is checked against three cases whose answers are known in advance: a perfect forecast scores 0 everywhere; a constant zero forecast scores WMAPE 100, WQL 1 and RQL 20; and a point forecast’s WQL equals its WMAPE/100.

Three naive baselines, which are not a formality

Always zero, repeat-last-week, and the mean of the last 28 days. On intermittent data the first is not absurd, and the third is a genuinely strong competitor — which is exactly why it is here.

Show the code 
baselines = ["zero", "seasonal_naive", "moving_average_28d"]
md(rename_models(table_of(baselines, "validation"))
   .select(["model", "n_series"] + metrics.METRICS).sort("WMAPE"))

Note the ordering: repeating last week is barely better than predicting nothing at all, while a flat 28-day mean removes about a fifth of the error. On series this sparse the weekly pattern of a single item is mostly noise, and averaging it away beats reproducing it.

RQL inverts the ranking — the zero forecast wins it outright, 20 against 39. That is the metric doing its job rather than a paradox: RQL prices over-prediction at four times under-prediction, and a forecast of zero can never over-predict.

The features

Rather than invent a feature set, this reproduces one that is public and placed well: btrotta/kaggle-m5 (predict.py), a top-4% M5 solution. Fifty-two features in four families:

  • the series’ own past — lags 1 to 7, means of the last 7/14/28 days, running mean and variance overall and per weekday;
  • the same, two levels up — the item’s department in its store, and the item across all stores, so a thin series borrows the shape of its neighbours;
  • calendar — weekday, month, SNAP benefit days, and an “event uplift” measured as mean sales n days after an event minus the same weekday a week later;
  • price — the current price and its change over 7 and 14 days, plus days since first sale.

The strategy is direct: 28 models, one per forecast day. The model for day k sees sales only up to the origin, with its lag features shifted k days further back, so no forecast is ever fed back as an input and nothing in the window leaks.

Five differences from the original are deliberate, and two of them handicap this reproduction: XGBoost instead of LightGBM with the closest equivalent settings; training on the 500-series sample rather than all 30,490 (the department and item features are still computed on all of them); boosting rounds chosen by early stopping on the last 28 days before the origin, where the original stops on the window it then reports; the event uplift implemented as intended rather than as coded; and missing days defined by the price file.

The second and third of those cost real accuracy. A reproduction that scored better than the original would be the suspicious outcome.

What it buys

    for role in ("validation", "test")

Show the code 
fig, axes = plots.plot_by_bucket(table_of(models, "validation", by="horizon_bucket"),
                                 metrics.METRICS)

Fifty-two features, 28 trained models and roughly 84 minutes of CPU at the test origin, against a flat mean of the last 28 days: 0.8 WMAPE points on validation, 1.2 on test. The horizon view says where that comes from — the two are nearly tied over the first fortnight, then the mean decays while the model does not, which is the one thing a model with a calendar in it ought to manage.

The per-series view is blunter still.

Show the code 
def per_series_wql(model):
    return metrics.score(scored(model, "validation"), by="id").select(
        "id", pl.col("WQL").alias(f"WQL_{model}"))

volume = (scored("xgboost_btrotta", "validation")
          .group_by("id").agg(pl.col("y").sum().alias("volume")))
comparison = (per_series_wql("moving_average_28d")
              .join(per_series_wql("xgboost_btrotta"), on="id")
              .join(volume, on="id"))

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(11, 3.5))
share = plots.index_plot(ax, comparison, "WQL",
                         "moving_average_28d", "xgboost_btrotta", order_by="volume")
plt.show()

XGBoost has the lower WQL on 43% of series. It loses on the majority of items and still wins overall, because the items it wins on are the ones that sell. Pooled metrics weight by volume; a per-series count does not. Both numbers are true, and quoting only the pooled one would be the kind of claim this harness exists to prevent.

What the baseline settles

Three things carry into the next post.

A number to beat. WMAPE 78.8 on validation, 76.4 on test — and the honest framing is that most of that was already available from a 28-day mean.

An axis on which to beat it. XGBoost returns a point forecast, so the comparison is MAE, WMAPE and P90. Its WQL is arithmetic, not a measurement.

A caveat that cuts the other way. XGBoost is trained on squared error, so it estimates a conditional mean. On absolute-error metrics the optimal point forecast is the median, so any model that returns a median starts with a structural advantage on MAE and WMAPE that has nothing to do with forecasting better. Chronos-2 returns a median. That has to be said before the comparison, not after it.
