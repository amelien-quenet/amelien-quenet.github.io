The previous post left a number to beat: WMAPE 78.8 on the validation window, from 52 hand-built features and 28 trained models. This post hands Chronos-2 the sales history and nothing else.

What the model is

Chronos-2 is a pretrained forecaster of about 120M parameters, a T5-style encoder. It cuts the history into patches of 16 days, embeds each as a vector, and emits the quantiles of the whole horizon in a single forward pass — no token vocabulary, no step-by-step decoding, and the horizon length is an input rather than a trained-in constant. Its point forecast is the median.

M5 was chosen partly because Chronos-2 has no training leakage on it, so a zero-shot number means something here.

Out of the box means: no covariates, no fine-tuning, cross-learning off, default context length. One detail about that default matters more than it looks. The model reads the last context_length days of a series and silently ignores the rest — no warning, no error. On daily M5 data the longest history at the validation origin is 1,885 days, shorter than the documented 2,048 default and far shorter than the 8,192 maximum, so every series is read in full. On a finer-grained grid the same 8,192 steps would be a much shorter span and the default would be quietly truncating most of the history.

Four checks before any number is trusted

Each of these can fail silently, and each would invalidate everything downstream:

  1. Batching is not sharing. With cross-learning off, a series forecast alone and the same series inside a batch of 100 must give the same answer. Largest observed difference: 3·10⁻⁷ units.
  2. The point forecast is the median. The library names the column predictions and computes it in a variable called mean. Largest observed \(\lvert \hat y - q_{0.5}\rvert\): exactly 0.
  3. The context length changes nothing yet. Forecasting at the 2,048 default must equal forecasting at the model maximum, since no history is that long. Difference: exactly 0.
  4. Quantiles ordered, no missing values. No missing values — but 19.1% of rows have at least one crossing quantile pair. That is the model’s own output, not a bug in the harness, and it is worth knowing before reading any interval as an interval.

MAE and WMAPE drop by 10.3% against XGBoost, with no features at all. The engineering that the gradient-boosted baseline needs — lags, rolling windows, per-weekday moments at three aggregation levels — the foundation model extracts from the raw history by reading it.

Two honest deductions from that headline. Part of the 10.3% is the median-versus-mean advantage named at the end of the previous post: XGBoost is trained on squared error and estimates a conditional mean, and on absolute-error metrics the median is the better point forecast regardless of which model is the better forecaster. And P90 goes the other way: Chronos-2 is 3.8% worse than XGBoost on the bad days. On the error you would actually staff for, the feature-engineered model is ahead.

Four reasonable ideas that made it worse

Every gain of the gradient-boosted baseline came from handing it information. Chronos-2 accepts extra inputs too, so the obvious next move is to hand it the same knowledge. The question is not rhetorical: anything derived from the timestamp or the target’s own past should be redundant to a model that reads the history, but prices, SNAP days and events are genuinely exogenous and cannot be inferred from sales alone.

Four families were tried, all on the validation window, all against zero-shot:

Read the table block by block.

Known covariates, one block at a time, all lose. Calendar costs 0.36% of WMAPE, price 0.47%, SNAP and events 0.16%. Weekday and month are the clearest case of redundancy — they are recoverable from the timestamps the model already has — but price and SNAP are not, and they lose anyway. Only all four blocks together helps, by 0.54%, which is small enough that a single validation window of 473 series cannot distinguish it from luck.

Cross-learning is the worst single idea tried: +0.85% WMAPE, +1.12% WQL, +3.79% RQL. Letting the series of a batch attend to each other should help a thin series borrow from its neighbours; on this data it hurts. One design note that makes the result about the mechanism rather than about a grouping: the series are sorted by id, so consecutive batches would be nearly single-department, and the order is randomised with a fixed seed before batching.

Every encoding of the long zero runs loses too. About 27% of history days sit inside a run of at least 14 consecutive zero-sale days while the item is on sale. Three ways to point them out: a past-only 0/1 covariate, a second multivariate target, and replacing the values with NaN so the model treats them as unobserved. The first two land at −0.08% WMAPE and +0.10% WQL; masking is the worst result in the study at +2.32% WMAPE and +1.99% WQL.

One trap had to be closed before any of that could be measured: the saved flags were computed on the whole dataset, so a zero run that straddles the origin was partly defined by days the model must not see. Recomputed on history alone, 307 history days change flag.

The diagnostic that inverted

Every run also reports the mean width of its 20–80% band, on the theory that an input the model cannot turn into a rule makes it less sure before it makes it worse. A widening would be the signature of a useless input.

That is not what happened. Seven of the eight attempts came back narrower than zero-shot while scoring worse — cross-learning narrowest of all, at −3.66%. The expected failure mode was hedging; the actual failure mode is confidence that is not earned. Only NaN-masking widened, by 11.3%, and it is also the one whose point forecast degraded most, which is at least self-consistent: told that a quarter of the history is unobserved, the model both loses the information and admits it.

Are the long zero runs predictable at all?

Before building anything on top of a forecast of them — a two-stage model, say — it is worth asking whether they can be forecast. Over the validation window, 2,000 of 13,244 scored (series, day) pairs are inside a real zero run. Two forecasts of them:

source days flagged precision recall
the multivariate is_blank channel, median ≥ 0.5 1,483 0.55 0.41
read off the zero-shot sales forecast 8,696 0.22 0.96

Neither is usable. And the second target has a deeper problem: it is only worth having if a forecast zero run pulls the sales forecast down. Among the 2,106 days where the model is confident of one, its sales forecast is above the zero-shot forecast 38% of the time. The two channels are barely coupled, so the multivariate framing is not merely unhelpful — on more than a third of the days where it fires, it is internally incoherent.

Context length: the one knob that matters, swept 46 ways

The context length is the single setting that decides how much history the model sees. More is not automatically better: a long context can dilute the recent behaviour that drives the next few days, while the far horizon may need more of the past. And because the far horizon and the near horizon may want different answers, the sweep is scored by horizon segment, not overall.

The decision rule was fixed before looking: for days 1–7 and for days 8–28 separately, keep the context with the lowest WQL. If the two differ, stitch — days 1–7 from one, days 8–28 from the other — with the boundary at the edge of the first horizon bucket rather than at a tuned value.

Show the code 
lengths = sorted(
    int(path.name.split("_")[1])
    for path in EXPERIMENTS.glob(f"context_*_{ROSTER}.parquet")
    if path.name.split("_")[1].isdigit() and "origin" not in path.name
)
segment = (pl.when(pl.col("horizon") <= 7).then(pl.lit("days 01-07"))
             .otherwise(pl.lit("days 08-28")).alias("segment"))
sweep = pl.concat([

                  by="segment").with_columns(pl.lit(length).alias("context_length"))
    for length in lengths
])

rows = []
for name in ("days 01-07", "days 08-28"):
    part = sweep.filter(pl.col("segment") == name)
    best = part.sort("WQL").row(0, named=True)
    plateau = part.filter(pl.col("WQL") <= best["WQL"] * 1.01)["context_length"].sort()
    rows.append({"segment": name, "best context": best["context_length"],

                 "lengths within 1%": len(plateau),
                 "shortest of those": int(plateau.min())})
md(pl.DataFrame(rows), digits=6)

The shape of that curve is the finding. Below roughly 150 days the model is badly starved and the error rises steeply; above a few hundred the curve is flat to within a fraction of a percent, and the chosen optimum sits on a plateau rather than at a point. Two confirmations that it really is a plateau: more than a dozen lengths fall within 1% of the winner in each segment, and the same selection rule run with WMAPE instead of WQL picks 1,288 for the early segment rather than 1,099 — the same flat region, a different point in it. A CPU run and a GPU run of this sweep disagreed on the coarse-grid winner and still selected the same final pair.

So: a 46-length sweep, a selection rule fixed in advance, a stitch across two regimes — and the whole exercise removes 0.11% of WMAPE and 0.08% of WQL. Reading the entire history, which is what the model does if you tell it nothing, is within a tenth of a percent of the best configuration available.

LoRA, aimed at the long horizon

Full fine-tuning would update 120M weights. LoRA freezes them and learns a rank-8 correction to the attention matrices and the output layer — a few hundred thousand trainable parameters, which is what makes the run affordable at all. Rank 8, learning rate 1e-5, 1,000 steps, effective batch 32.

The protocol keeps the fine-tune away from what it is scored on: training windows are cut at random from history up to origin − 28 days, the 28 days immediately before the origin are the validation target scored every 100 steps, the best checkpoint by that loss is kept, and the forecast window is never touched. The test-origin model is a fresh fine-tune with the same configuration on the longer history, exactly as the baseline was retrained per window.

Since nothing forces a model to forecast at the context length it was tuned at, all four pairings were tried.

Show the code 
def lora(finetune_context, predict_context, origin):
    name = (f"lora_ctx{finetune_context}_steps1000_lr1e-05_bs32"
            f"_context_{predict_context}_origin{origin}")
    return experiment(name)

configs = {f"zero-shot, context {c}": experiment(f"context_{c}") for c in (early, late)}
for finetune_context in (late, early):
    for predict_context in (early, late):
        configs[f"LoRA@{finetune_context}, context {predict_context}"] = lora(
            finetune_context, predict_context, VALIDATION_ORIGIN)

by_segment = metrics.compare(
    {name: on_validation(table).with_columns(segment) for name, table in configs.items()},
    by="segment")
md(by_segment.select("model", "segment", "WQL").pivot(on="segment", index="model",
                                                      values="WQL"), digits=6)

Per-segment winners: fine-tuned at 1,099 and forecasting at 1,099 for days 1–7, fine-tuned at 1,099 and forecasting at 1,829 for days 8–28. The long segment is a cross pairing — tuned at one length, predicting at another — which training and predicting at a single length would never have surfaced. It is also the kind of result that is easy to over-read: the gap between it and the next configuration is in the fourth decimal of WQL.

The cost is worth recording. On the laptop CPU this measured 21.7 s per training step and 126 s per evaluation pass: 6.4 hours per fine-tune, up to 25.5 hours for the plan. The same work on a GTX 1080 took about 32 minutes per run. Every number in this section that is compared with another was produced on the same device, because CPU and GPU disagree in the third decimal of WQL — enough to invert a ranking this tight.

What this adds up to

Out of the box, on data it had never seen, Chronos-2 beat a reproduced top-4% feature pipeline by 8.3% of WMAPE. Then: four families of extra information, a 46-length sweep of its most important setting, a stitch across horizon segments and a LoRA fine-tune — and the combined effect of all of it is a fraction of a percent, in the wrong direction on the point forecast.

The flat part of that story is not a failure of effort; it is a measurement. Every configuration tried lands in the same narrow band, which is what a ceiling looks like from the inside. The honest next question is not “which knob was missed” but “is this a limit of the tuning, or of the problem?” — and that is answerable, which is what the third post does.

One lever is left untried here, and it is the one worth testing properly: treat different series differently — fine-tune per group, cross-learn inside a group, one model per group. That has a precondition which can be tested before any of it is built.

```{python}
runs = {"zero-shot": scored("chronos2_vanilla", "validation")}
for label, filename in attempt_files.items():
    table = experiment(filename)
    if "target_name" in table.columns:          # the multivariate run saves both channels
        table = table.filter(pl.col("target_name") == "sales").drop("target_name")
    runs[label] = on_validation(table)

changes = metrics.compare(runs, reference="zero-shot")
widths = pl.DataFrame({
    "model": list(runs),
    "width": [t.select((pl.col("q0.8") - pl.col("q0.2")).mean()).item() for t in runs.values()],
})
reference_width = widths.filter(pl.col("model") == "zero-shot")["width"][0]
widths = widths.with_columns(
    (100 * (pl.col("width") / reference_width - 1)).alias("width_change_%"))

md(changes.join(widths.drop("width"), on="model")
   .filter(pl.col("model") != "zero-shot")
   .select("model", "WMAPE_change_%", "P90_change_%", "WQL_change_%", "width_change_%"))
```

```{python}
#| code-fold: true

fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(12, 0.45 * attempts.height + 1.2), sharey=True)
plots.plot_change_bars(axes[0], attempts, "WQL")
```

Read the table block by block.

**Known covariates, one block at a time, all lose.** Calendar costs 0.36% of WMAPE, price
0.47%, SNAP and events 0.16%. Weekday and month are the clearest case of redundancy — they
are recoverable from the timestamps the model already has — but price and SNAP are not, and
they lose anyway. Only *all four blocks together* helps, by 0.54%, which is small enough
that a single validation window of 473 series cannot distinguish it from luck.

**Cross-learning is the worst single idea tried**: +0.85% WMAPE, +1.12% WQL, +3.79% RQL.
Letting the series of a batch attend to each other should help a thin series borrow from its
neighbours; on this data it hurts. One design note that makes the result about the mechanism
rather than about a grouping: the series are sorted by id, so consecutive batches would be
nearly single-department, and the order is randomised with a fixed seed before batching.

**Every encoding of the long zero runs loses too.** About 27% of history days sit inside a
run of at least 14 consecutive zero-sale days while the item is on sale. Three ways to point
them out: a past-only 0/1 covariate, a second multivariate target, and replacing the values
with NaN so the model treats them as unobserved. The first two land at −0.08% WMAPE and
+0.10% WQL; masking is the worst result in the study at +2.32% WMAPE and +1.99% WQL.

One trap had to be closed before any of that could be measured: the saved flags were
computed on the whole dataset, so a zero run that straddles the origin was partly defined by
days the model must not see. Recomputed on history alone, 307 history days change flag.

### The diagnostic that inverted

Every run also reports the mean width of its 20–80% band, on the theory that *an input the
model cannot turn into a rule makes it less sure before it makes it worse*. A widening would
be the signature of a useless input.

That is not what happened. Seven of the eight attempts came back **narrower** than zero-shot
while scoring worse — cross-learning narrowest of all, at −3.66%. The expected failure mode
was hedging; the actual failure mode is **confidence that is not earned**. Only NaN-masking
widened, by 11.3%, and it is also the one whose point forecast degraded most, which is at
least self-consistent: told that a quarter of the history is unobserved, the model both
loses the information and admits it.

### Are the long zero runs predictable at all?

Before building anything on top of a forecast of them — a two-stage model, say — it is worth
asking whether they can be forecast. Over the validation window, 2,000 of 13,244 scored
(series, day) pairs are inside a real zero run. Two forecasts of them:

| source | days flagged | precision | recall |
|---|---|---|---|
| the multivariate `is_blank` channel, median ≥ 0.5 | 1,483 | 0.55 | 0.41 |
| read off the zero-shot sales forecast | 8,696 | 0.22 | 0.96 |

Neither is usable. And the second target has a deeper problem: it is only worth having if a
forecast zero run pulls the sales forecast down. Among the 2,106 days where the model is
confident of one, its sales forecast is **above** the zero-shot forecast 38% of the time. The
two channels are barely coupled, so the multivariate framing is not merely unhelpful — on
more than a third of the days where it fires, it is internally incoherent.

## Context length: the one knob that matters, swept 46 ways

The context length is the single setting that decides how much history the model sees. More
is not automatically better: a long context can dilute the recent behaviour that drives the
next few days, while the far horizon may need more of the past. And because the far horizon
and the near horizon may want different answers, the sweep is scored **by horizon segment**,
not overall.

The decision rule was fixed before looking: for days 1–7 and for days 8–28 separately, keep
the context with the lowest WQL. If the two differ, *stitch* — days 1–7 from one, days 8–28
from the other — with the boundary at the edge of the first horizon bucket rather than at a
tuned value.

```{python}
#| label: fig-sweep
#| fig-cap: "WQL against context length, per horizon segment, relative to reading the whole history. Below zero: better than zero-shot. 46 lengths, log scale."
#| code-fold: true
lengths = sorted(
    int(path.name.split("_")[1])
    for path in EXPERIMENTS.glob(f"context_*_{ROSTER}.parquet")
    if path.name.split("_")[1].isdigit() and "origin" not in path.name
)
segment = (pl.when(pl.col("horizon") <= 7).then(pl.lit("days 01-07"))
             .otherwise(pl.lit("days 08-28")).alias("segment"))
sweep = pl.concat([
    metrics.score(on_validation(experiment(f"context_{length}")).with_columns(segment),
                  by="segment").with_columns(pl.lit(length).alias("context_length"))
    for length in lengths
])

longest = max(lengths)
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(11, 3.5))
for name, colour in [("days 01-07", plots.MAIN_COLOR), ("days 08-28", plots.TEXT_COLOR)]:
    part = sweep.filter(pl.col("segment") == name).sort("context_length")
    reference = part.filter(pl.col("context_length") == longest)["WQL"][0]
    ax.plot(part["context_length"].to_numpy(),
            100 * (part["WQL"].to_numpy() / reference - 1),
            color=colour, marker="o", markersize=4, linewidth=1.5, label=name)
ax.axhline(0, color=plots.MUTED_TEXT_COLOR, linewidth=1)
ax.set_xscale("log")
ax.set_xlabel("context length (days, log scale)")
ax.set_ylabel("WQL vs whole history (%)")
ax.legend()
plt.show()
```

```{python}
#| label: tbl-sweep
#| tbl-cap: "The winner per segment, and every length within 1% of it."
#| code-fold: true
rows = []
for name in ("days 01-07", "days 08-28"):
    part = sweep.filter(pl.col("segment") == name)
    best = part.sort("WQL").row(0, named=True)
    plateau = part.filter(pl.col("WQL") <= best["WQL"] * 1.01)["context_length"].sort()
    rows.append({"segment": name, "best context": best["context_length"],
                 "WQL": round(best["WQL"], 6),
                 "lengths within 1%": len(plateau),
                 "shortest of those": int(plateau.min())})
md(pl.DataFrame(rows), digits=6)
```

The shape of that curve is the finding. Below roughly 150 days the model is badly starved
and the error rises steeply; above a few hundred the curve is **flat to within a fraction of
a percent**, and the chosen optimum sits on a plateau rather than at a point. Two
confirmations that it really is a plateau: more than a dozen lengths fall within 1% of the
winner in each segment, and the same selection rule run with WMAPE instead of WQL picks
1,288 for the early segment rather than 1,099 — the same flat region, a different point in
it. A CPU run and a GPU run of this sweep disagreed on the coarse-grid winner and still
selected the same final pair.

```{python}
#| label: tbl-stitch
#| tbl-cap: "The stitch against reading the whole history, validation window."
#| code-fold: true
choice = read_json("context_choice")
early, late = choice["early_context"], choice["late_context"]
from m5_chronos2.chronos2 import stitch

candidates = {
    "whole history": experiment(f"context_{longest}"),
    f"best single length ({late})": experiment(f"context_{late}"),
    f"stitch {early} -> {late}": stitch(experiment(f"context_{early}"),
                                        experiment(f"context_{late}"),
                                        choice["early_last_day"]),
}
md(metrics.compare({k: on_validation(v) for k, v in candidates.items()},
                   reference="whole history")
   .select(["model"] + [f"{m}_change_%" for m in metrics.METRICS]), digits=3)
```

So: a 46-length sweep, a selection rule fixed in advance, a stitch across two regimes — and
the whole exercise removes **0.11% of WMAPE and 0.08% of WQL.** Reading the entire history,
which is what the model does if you tell it nothing, is within a tenth of a percent of the
best configuration available.

## LoRA, aimed at the long horizon

Full fine-tuning would update 120M weights. LoRA freezes them and learns a rank-8 correction
to the attention matrices and the output layer — a few hundred thousand trainable parameters,
which is what makes the run affordable at all. Rank 8, learning rate 1e-5, 1,000 steps,
effective batch 32.

The protocol keeps the fine-tune away from what it is scored on: training windows are cut at
random from history up to *origin − 28 days*, the 28 days immediately before the origin are
the validation target scored every 100 steps, the best checkpoint by that loss is kept, and
the forecast window is never touched. The test-origin model is a fresh fine-tune with the
same configuration on the longer history, exactly as the baseline was retrained per window.

Since nothing forces a model to forecast at the context length it was tuned at, all four
pairings were tried.

```{python}
#| label: tbl-lora
#| tbl-cap: "Validation WQL per segment. Lower is better; the two winners are the configuration carried forward."
#| code-fold: true
def lora(finetune_context, predict_context, origin):
    name = (f"lora_ctx{finetune_context}_steps1000_lr1e-05_bs32"
            f"_context_{predict_context}_origin{origin}")
    return experiment(name)

configs = {f"zero-shot, context {c}": experiment(f"context_{c}") for c in (early, late)}
for finetune_context in (late, early):
    for predict_context in (early, late):
        configs[f"LoRA@{finetune_context}, context {predict_context}"] = lora(
            finetune_context, predict_context, VALIDATION_ORIGIN)

by_segment = metrics.compare(
    {name: on_validation(table).with_columns(segment) for name, table in configs.items()},
    by="segment")
md(by_segment.select("model", "segment", "WQL").pivot(on="segment", index="model",
                                                      values="WQL"), digits=6)
```

Per-segment winners: **fine-tuned at 1,099 and forecasting at 1,099 for days 1–7, fine-tuned
at 1,099 and forecasting at 1,829 for days 8–28.** The long segment is a *cross* pairing —
tuned at one length, predicting at another — which training and predicting at a single length
would never have surfaced. It is also the kind of result that is easy to over-read: the gap
between it and the next configuration is in the fourth decimal of WQL.

The cost is worth recording. On the laptop CPU this measured 21.7 s per training step and
126 s per evaluation pass: 6.4 hours per fine-tune, up to 25.5 hours for the plan. The same
work on a GTX 1080 took about 32 minutes per run. Every number in this section that is
compared with another was produced on the same device, because CPU and GPU disagree in the
third decimal of WQL — enough to invert a ranking this tight.

## What this adds up to

Out of the box, on data it had never seen, Chronos-2 beat a reproduced top-4% feature
pipeline by 8.3% of WMAPE. Then: four families of extra information, a 46-length sweep of its
most important setting, a stitch across horizon segments and a LoRA fine-tune — and the
combined effect of all of it is **a fraction of a percent, in the wrong direction on the
point forecast.**

The flat part of that story is not a failure of effort; it is a measurement. Every
configuration tried lands in the same narrow band, which is what a ceiling looks like from
the inside. The honest next question is not "which knob was missed" but **"is this a limit of
the tuning, or of the problem?"** — and that is answerable, which is what [the third
post](../03-ceiling/) does.

One lever is left untried here, and it is the one worth testing properly: **treat different
series differently** — fine-tune per group, cross-learn inside a group, one model per group.
That has a precondition which can be tested before any of it is built.
