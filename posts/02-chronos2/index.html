The previous post left me a number to beat: WMAPE 78.8 on the validation window, from 52 hand-built features and 28 trained models. Here I hand Chronos-2 the sales history and nothing else.

What the model is

Chronos-2 is a pretrained forecaster of about 120M parameters, a T5-style encoder. It cuts the history into patches of 16 days, embeds each as a vector, and emits the quantiles of the whole horizon in a single forward pass — no token vocabulary, no step-by-step decoding, and the horizon length is an input rather than a trained-in constant. Its point forecast is the median.

The original project behind Chronos-2, at Amazon, was to use an LLM to predict inside the “language” of time series. But building a vocabulary for real numbers turned out to be inefficient, which led the team to strip both the first and the last layer of the architecture and keep only the matrix algebra in between.

I chose M5 partly because Chronos-2 has no training leakage on it, so a zero-shot number is an authentic statement.

Out of the box means: no covariates, no fine-tuning, cross-learning off, default context length. One detail about that default matters more than it looks. The model reads the last context_length days of a series and silently ignores the rest — no warning, no error. On daily M5 data the longest history at the validation origin is 1,885 days, shorter than the documented 2,048 default and far shorter than the 8,192 maximum, so every series is read in full. On a finer-grained grid the same 8,192 steps would be a much shorter span and the default would be quietly truncating most of the history.

Four prior checks made

Each of these can fail silently, and each would invalidate everything downstream:

  1. Batching is not sharing. With cross-learning off, a series forecast alone and the same series inside a batch of 100 must give the same answer.
  2. The point forecast is the median. The library names the column predictions and computes it in a variable misleadingly called mean.
  3. The context length changes nothing yet. Forecasting at the 2,048 default must equal forecasting at the model maximum, since no history is that long.
  4. Quantiles ordered, no missing values. No missing values — but 19.1% of rows have at least one crossing quantile pair. That is the model’s own output, not a bug in the harness, and it is worth knowing before reading any interval as an interval.

MAE and WMAPE drop by 10.3% against XGBoost, with no features at all. The engineering the gradient-boosted baseline needs — lags, rolling windows, per-weekday moments at three aggregation levels — is handmade, and therefore crude: reality does not line up with it that neatly. Chronos-2 works from a far richer representation, and it was never shown this kind of series while it was being built. That is the part I find impressive.

Two honest deductions from that headline. Part of the 10.3% is the median-versus-mean advantage named at the end of the previous post: XGBoost is trained on squared error and estimates a conditional mean, and on absolute-error metrics the median is the better point forecast regardless of which model is the better forecaster. And P90 goes the other way: Chronos-2 is 3.8% worse than XGBoost on the bad days. On the error you would actually staff for, the feature-engineered model is ahead.

Four reasonable but inadequate ideas

Every gain of the gradient-boosted baseline came from handing it information. Chronos-2 accepts extra inputs too, so the obvious next move is to hand it the same knowledge. The question is not rhetorical. Anything derived from the target’s own past should be redundant to a model that reads the history. But prices, SNAP days and events are genuinely exogenous and cannot be inferred from sales alone — and neither can the calendar, since Chronos-2 reads only the target values and so has no way of knowing whether a given day is Christmas or just another Monday.

I tried four families, all on the validation window, all against zero-shot:

Read the table block by block.

Known covariates, one block at a time, all lose. Calendar costs 0.36% of WMAPE, price 0.47%, SNAP and events 0.16%. Weekday and month are the clearest case of redundancy — they are recoverable from the timestamps the model already has — but price and SNAP are not, and they lose anyway. Only all four blocks together helps, by 0.54%, which is small enough that a single validation window of 473 series cannot distinguish it from luck.

Cross-learning is the worst single idea tried: +0.85% WMAPE, +1.12% WQL, +3.79% RQL. Letting the series of a batch attend to each other should help a thin series borrow from its neighbours; on this data it hurts. One design note that makes the result about the mechanism rather than about a grouping: the series are sorted by id, so consecutive batches would be nearly single-department, and the order is randomised with a fixed seed before batching.

Every encoding of the long zero runs loses too. About 27% of history days sit inside a run of at least 14 consecutive zero-sale days while the item is on sale. Three ways to point them out: a past-only 0/1 covariate, a second multivariate target, and replacing the values with NaN so the model treats them as unobserved. The first two land at −0.08% WMAPE and +0.10% WQL; masking is the worst result in the study at +2.32% WMAPE and +1.99% WQL.

One trap I had to close before any of that could be measured: the saved flags were computed on the whole dataset, so a zero run straddling the origin was partly defined by days the model must not see.

If there is a single takeaway from these eight runs, it is that with a foundation model feature engineering harms predictive power: it brute-forces a crude characterisation onto far subtler machinery. That is the opposite of an algorithm like XGBoost, where the more the better, because it simply never uses poor metadata — gain optimisation being the greedy backbone of its decision trees.

What about uncertainty?

I also recorded the mean width of every run’s 20–80% band, on the theory that an input the model cannot turn into a rule makes it less sure before it makes it worse. A widening would be the signature of a useless input.

That is not what happened. 7 of the 8 attempts came back narrower than zero-shot while scoring worse — cross-learning narrowest of all, at -3.66%. The expected failure mode was hedging; the actual failure mode is confidence that is not earned. Only NaN-masking widened, by 11.3%, and it is also the one whose point forecast degraded most, which is at least self-consistent: told that a quarter of the history is unobserved, the model both loses the information and admits it.

Are the long zero runs predictable at all?

Before building anything on top of a forecast of them — a two-stage model, say — it is worth asking whether they can be forecast at all. The second of those three encodings makes that easy to ask, once one thing about the model is said plainly.

Chronos-2 can forecast several related series jointly. So instead of asking it for sales alone, that run asked it for two channels per item:

  • sales, in units — the thing actually being forecast;
  • is_blank, a 0/1 flag — is this day inside a long zero run?

It returns a forecast of both for every day of the window. That gives two things to check: whether the blank channel is any good by itself, and whether it pushes the sales channel in the right direction.

By itself, it is not good. Over the validation window, 2,000 of 13,244 scored (series, day) pairs sit inside a real zero run:

source days flagged precision recall
the is_blank channel, median ≥ 0.5 1,483 0.55 0.41
read off the zero-shot sales forecast 8,696 0.22 0.96

Neither is usable: the channel misses three fifths of the runs it should catch, and reading them off the sales forecast flags so many days that the flag means nothing.

The deeper problem is the second check. The extra channel is only worth having if a forecast zero run drags the sales forecast down. That is directly testable, by comparing two sales forecasts of the same item on the same day, from two runs differing in exactly one respect:

  • without the blank channel — Chronos-2 given the sales history and nothing else, which is the zero-shot run everything in this post is measured against;
  • with it — the same model, the same history, plus the blank-run channel alongside.

Keep only the days where the run’s own blank channel predicts a zero run, and every point should fall below the diagonal.

They do not. On 35% of those 1,483 days, knowing about the zero run made the model forecast more sales, not fewer. It is asserting “a long zero run is likely here” and “I now expect higher sales here than I would have without that knowledge” at the same time. The two channels are barely coupled, so the multivariate framing is not merely unhelpful: a third of the times it fires, it contradicts itself.

Context length: the one knob that matters, swept 46 ways

The context length is the single setting that decides how much history the model sees. More is not automatically better: a long context can dilute the recent behaviour that drives the next few days, while the far horizon may need more of the past. And because the far horizon and the near horizon may want different answers, the sweep is scored by horizon segment, not overall.

I fixed the decision rule before looking: for days 1–7 and for days 8–28 separately, keep the context with the lowest WQL. If the two differ, stitch — days 1–7 from one, days 8–28 from the other — with the boundary at the edge of the first horizon bucket rather than at a tuned value.

Show the code 
lengths = sorted(
    int(path.name.split("_")[1])
    for path in EXPERIMENTS.glob(f"context_*_{ROSTER}.parquet")
    if path.name.split("_")[1].isdigit() and "origin" not in path.name
)
segment = (pl.when(pl.col("horizon") <= 7).then(pl.lit("days 01-07"))
             .otherwise(pl.lit("days 08-28")).alias("segment"))
sweep = pl.concat([
    metrics.score(on_validation(experiment(f"context_{length}")).with_columns(segment),
                  by="segment").with_columns(pl.lit(length).alias("context_length"))
    for length in lengths
])

longest = max(lengths)
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(11, 3.5))
for name, colour in [("days 01-07", plots.MAIN_COLOR), ("days 08-28", plots.TEXT_COLOR)]:
    part = sweep.filter(pl.col("segment") == name).sort("context_length")
    reference = part.filter(pl.col("context_length") == longest)["WQL"][0]
    ax.plot(part["context_length"].to_numpy(),
            100 * (part["WQL"].to_numpy() / reference - 1),
            color=colour, marker="o", markersize=4, linewidth=1.5, label=name)
ax.axhline(0, color=plots.MUTED_TEXT_COLOR, linewidth=1)
ax.set_xscale("log")
ax.set_xlabel("context length (days, log scale)")
ax.set_ylabel("WQL vs whole history (%)")
ax.legend()
plt.show()

The shape of that curve is the finding. Below roughly 150 days the model is badly starved and the error rises steeply; above a few hundred it is flat to within a fraction of a percent. The best length is 1,099 days for the first week and 1,829 for days 8–28 — but neither is a point, it is a plateau: 33 and 31 lengths score within 1% of those winners.

Two more things say the same. The same selection rule run on WMAPE instead of WQL picks 1,288 for the early segment rather than 1,099 — the same flat region, a different point in it. And a CPU run and a GPU run of the sweep disagreed on the coarse-grid winner, yet still selected the same final pair.

So: a 46-length sweep, a selection rule fixed in advance, a stitch across two regimes — and the whole exercise removes 0.11% of WMAPE and 0.08% of WQL. Reading the entire history, which is what the model does if you tell it nothing, is within a tenth of a percent of the best configuration available. Remember, though, that in this setting no series was longer than 2,000 days, which is far from the maximum capacity of Chronos-2: on a different model, or with more history, the relationship between performance and context length could be non-monotonic.

LoRA for finetuning

Full fine-tuning would update 120M weights. LoRA (for low rank) freezes them and learns a low-rank correction to the attention matrices and the output layer — a few hundred thousand trainable parameters, which is what makes the run affordable at all. I used rank 8, learning rate 1e-5, 1,000 steps, effective batch 32.

The protocol keeps the fine-tune away from what it is scored on:

Nothing forces a model to forecast at the context length it was tuned at, so I tried all four pairings.

Show the code 
def lora(finetune_context, predict_context, origin):
    name = (f"lora_ctx{finetune_context}_steps1000_lr1e-05_bs32"
            f"_context_{predict_context}_origin{origin}")
    return experiment(name)

configs = {f"zero-shot, context {c}": experiment(f"context_{c}") for c in (early, late)}
for finetune_context in (late, early):
    for predict_context in (early, late):
        configs[f"LoRA@{finetune_context}, context {predict_context}"] = lora(
            finetune_context, predict_context, VALIDATION_ORIGIN)

by_segment = metrics.compare(
    {name: on_validation(table).with_columns(segment) for name, table in configs.items()},
    by="segment")
md(by_segment.select("model", "segment", "WQL").pivot(on="segment", index="model",
                                                      values="WQL"), digits=6)

Per-segment winners: fine-tuned at 1,099 and forecasting at 1,099 for days 1–7, fine-tuned at 1,099 and forecasting at 1,829 for days 8–28. The long segment is a cross pairing — tuned at one length, predicting at another — which training and predicting at a single length would never have surfaced. It is also the kind of result that is easy to over-read: the gap between it and the next configuration is in the fourth decimal of WQL.

The cost is worth recording. On my laptop CPU this measured 21.7 s per training step and 126 s per evaluation pass: 6.4 hours per fine-tune, up to 25.5 hours for the whole plan. The same work on a GTX 1080 took about 32 minutes per run. Every number in this section that I compare with another was produced on the same device, because CPU and GPU disagree in the third decimal of WQL — enough to invert a ranking this tight.

What this adds up to

Out of the box, on data it had never seen, Chronos-2 beat a reproduced top-4% feature pipeline by 8.3% of WMAPE. Then: four families of extra information, a 46-length sweep of its most important setting, a stitch across horizon segments and a LoRA fine-tune — and the combined effect of all of it is a fraction of a percent, in the wrong direction on the point forecast. These results really highlight the power of the model’s architecture — it isn’t even a heavy-duty product with its 120M parameters, compared to GPT-3’s 175B parameters: and that was in 2020!

The flat part of that story is not a failure of effort. Every configuration I tried lands in the same narrow band, which is what a ceiling looks like from the inside. The honest next question is not “which knob did I miss” but “is this a limit of the tuning, or of the problem?” — and that one is answerable, which is what the third post does. It also weighs the one lever I never pulled: treat different series differently — fine-tune per group, cross-learn inside a group, one model per group. The possibilities there are endless, which is exactly why the question deserves an answer before any of them is built.

history and nothing else.

## What the model is

Chronos-2 is a pretrained forecaster of about 120M parameters, a T5-style encoder. It cuts
the history into patches of 16 days, embeds each as a vector, and emits the quantiles of the
*whole* horizon in a single forward pass — no token vocabulary, no step-by-step decoding,
and the horizon length is an input rather than a trained-in constant. Its point forecast is
the median.

The original project behind Chronos-2, at Amazon, was to use an LLM to predict inside the
"language" of time series. But building a vocabulary for real numbers turned out to be
inefficient, which led the team to strip both the first and the last layer of the
architecture and keep only the matrix algebra in between.

I chose M5 partly because Chronos-2 has no training leakage on it, so a zero-shot number is
an authentic statement.

Out of the box means: no covariates, no fine-tuning, cross-learning off, default context
length. One detail about that default matters more than it looks. The model reads the last
`context_length` days of a series and **silently ignores the rest** — no warning, no error.
On daily M5 data the longest history at the validation origin is 1,885 days, shorter than
the documented 2,048 default and far shorter than the 8,192 maximum, so every series is read
in full. On a finer-grained grid the same 8,192 steps would be a much shorter span and the
default would be quietly truncating most of the history.

## Four prior checks made

Each of these can fail silently, and each would invalidate everything downstream:

1. **Batching is not sharing.** With cross-learning off, a series forecast alone and the
   same series inside a batch of 100 must give the same answer.
2. **The point forecast is the median.** The library names the column `predictions` and
   computes it in a variable misleadingly called `mean`.
3. **The context length changes nothing yet.** Forecasting at the 2,048 default must equal
   forecasting at the model maximum, since no history is that long.
4. **Quantiles ordered, no missing values.** No missing values — but **19.1% of rows have at
   least one crossing quantile pair.** That is the model's own output, not a bug in the
   harness, and it is worth knowing before reading any interval as an interval.

## Four reasonable but inadequate ideas

Every gain of the gradient-boosted baseline came from handing it information. Chronos-2
accepts extra inputs too, so the obvious next move is to hand it the same knowledge. The
question is not rhetorical. Anything derived from the target's own past *should* be redundant
to a model that reads the history. But prices, SNAP days and events are genuinely exogenous
and cannot be inferred from sales alone — and neither can the calendar, since Chronos-2 reads
only the target values and so has no way of knowing whether a given day is Christmas or just
another Monday.

I tried four families, all on the validation window, all against zero-shot:

```{python}
#| echo: false
attempt_files = {

    "price": "known_price",
```


Read the table block by block.

**Known covariates, one block at a time, all lose.** Calendar costs 0.36% of WMAPE, price
0.47%, SNAP and events 0.16%. Weekday and month are the clearest case of redundancy — they
are recoverable from the timestamps the model already has — but price and SNAP are not, and
they lose anyway. Only *all four blocks together* helps, by 0.54%, which is small enough
that a single validation window of 473 series cannot distinguish it from luck.

**Cross-learning is the worst single idea tried**: +0.85% WMAPE, +1.12% WQL, +3.79% RQL.
Letting the series of a batch attend to each other should help a thin series borrow from its
neighbours; on this data it hurts. One design note that makes the result about the mechanism
rather than about a grouping: the series are sorted by id, so consecutive batches would be
nearly single-department, and the order is randomised with a fixed seed before batching.

**Every encoding of the long zero runs loses too.** About 27% of history days sit inside a
run of at least 14 consecutive zero-sale days while the item is on sale. Three ways to point
them out: a past-only 0/1 covariate, a second multivariate target, and replacing the values
with NaN so the model treats them as unobserved. The first two land at −0.08% WMAPE and
+0.10% WQL; masking is the worst result in the study at +2.32% WMAPE and +1.99% WQL.

One trap I had to close before any of that could be measured: the saved flags were computed
on the whole dataset, so a zero run straddling the origin was partly defined by days the
model must not see.

If there is a single **takeaway** from these eight runs, it is that with a foundation model
**feature engineering harms predictive power**: it brute-forces a crude characterisation onto
far subtler machinery. That is the opposite of an algorithm like XGBoost, where **the more the
better**, because it simply never uses poor metadata — gain optimisation being the greedy
backbone of its decision trees.

### What about uncertainty?

I also recorded the mean width of every run's 20–80% band, on the theory that *an input the
model cannot turn into a rule makes it less sure before it makes it worse*. A widening would
be the signature of a useless input.

That is not what happened. `{python} f"{n_narrower}"` of the `{python} f"{band.height}"`
attempts came back **narrower** than zero-shot while scoring worse — cross-learning narrowest
of all, at `{python} f"{narrowest['width_change_%']:.2f}"`%. The expected failure mode was
hedging; the actual failure mode is **confidence that is not earned**. Only NaN-masking
widened, by `{python} f"{widest['width_change_%']:.1f}"`%, and it is also the one whose point
forecast degraded most, which is at least self-consistent: told that a quarter of the history
is unobserved, the model both loses the information and admits it.

### Are the long zero runs predictable at all?

Before building anything on top of a forecast of them — a two-stage model, say — it is worth
asking whether they can be forecast at all. The second of those three encodings makes that
easy to ask, once one thing about the model is said plainly.

Chronos-2 can forecast several related series jointly. So instead of asking it for sales
alone, that run asked it for **two channels per item**:

- **sales**, in units — the thing actually being forecast;
- **`is_blank`**, a 0/1 flag — *is this day inside a long zero run?*

It returns a forecast of both for every day of the window. That gives two things to check:
whether the blank channel is any good by itself, and whether it pushes the sales channel in
the right direction.

**By itself, it is not good.** Over the validation window, 2,000 of 13,244 scored
(series, day) pairs sit inside a real zero run:

| source | days flagged | precision | recall |
|---|---|---|---|
| the `is_blank` channel, median ≥ 0.5 | 1,483 | 0.55 | 0.41 |
| read off the zero-shot sales forecast | 8,696 | 0.22 | 0.96 |

Neither is usable: the channel misses three fifths of the runs it should catch, and reading
them off the sales forecast flags so many days that the flag means nothing.

**The deeper problem is the second check.** The extra channel is only worth having if a
forecast zero run drags the sales forecast **down**. That is directly testable, by comparing
two sales forecasts of the same item on the same day, from two runs differing in exactly one
respect:

- **without the blank channel** — Chronos-2 given the sales history and nothing else, which
  is the zero-shot run everything in this post is measured against;
- **with it** — the same model, the same history, plus the blank-run channel alongside.

Keep only the days where the run's own blank channel predicts a zero run, and every point
should fall below the diagonal.

```{python}
#| label: fig-coupling
#| fig-cap: "Two sales forecasts of the same item on the same day, from two runs differing only in whether Chronos-2 was also given the blank-run channel. Restricted to the days that channel predicts a zero run (median ≥ 0.5). A channel doing its job would put every point below the diagonal."
#| code-fold: true
multivariate = experiment("blank_second_target_both_channels")
blank_median = (multivariate.filter(pl.col("target_name") == "is_blank")
                .select("id", "origin", "date", pl.col("q0.5").alias("blank_median")))
vanilla = pl.read_parquet(ARTIFACTS / "predictions" / f"chronos2_vanilla_{ROSTER}.parquet")

coupling = (
    on_validation(multivariate.filter(pl.col("target_name") == "sales")
                  .drop("target_name"))
    .select("id", "origin", "date", pl.col("y_pred").alias("second_target"))
    .join(blank_median, on=["id", "origin", "date"])
    .join(on_validation(vanilla).select("id", "origin", "date",
                                        pl.col("y_pred").alias("zero_shot")),
          on=["id", "origin", "date"])
)
fires = coupling.filter(pl.col("blank_median") >= 0.5)
x = fires["zero_shot"].to_numpy()
y = fires["second_target"].to_numpy()
n_fires = fires.height
share_above = float((y > x).mean())

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(6.2, 4.2))
limit = float(max(x.max(), y.max())) * 1.05
ax.plot([0, limit], [0, limit], color=plots.TEXT_COLOR, linewidth=1, zorder=3)
ax.scatter(x[y <= x], y[y <= x], s=9, alpha=0.30, color=plots.MUTED_TEXT_COLOR,
           edgecolor="none", label="blank channel pulled sales down (as intended)")
ax.scatter(x[y > x], y[y > x], s=9, alpha=0.45, color=plots.MAIN_COLOR,
           edgecolor="none", label=f"it pushed them up ({share_above:.0%})")
ax.set_xlim(0, limit); ax.set_ylim(0, limit)
ax.set_xlabel("sales forecast WITHOUT the blank channel (units)")
ax.set_ylabel("sales forecast WITH it (units)")
ax.legend(fontsize=8, frameon=False, loc="upper left", markerscale=2)
fig.tight_layout()
plt.show()
```

They do not. On `{python} f"{share_above:.0%}"` of those `{python} f"{n_fires:,}"` days,
knowing about the zero run made the model forecast **more** sales, not fewer. It is asserting
*"a long zero run is likely here"* and *"I now expect higher sales here than I would have
without that knowledge"* at the same time. The two channels are barely coupled, so the
multivariate framing is not merely unhelpful: a third of the times it fires, it contradicts
itself.

## Context length: the one knob that matters, swept 46 ways

The context length is the single setting that decides how much history the model sees. More
is not automatically better: a long context can dilute the recent behaviour that drives the
next few days, while the far horizon may need more of the past. And because the far horizon
and the near horizon may want different answers, the sweep is scored **by horizon segment**,
not overall.

I fixed the decision rule before looking: for days 1–7 and for days 8–28 separately, keep the
context with the lowest WQL. If the two differ, *stitch* — days 1–7 from one, days 8–28 from
the other — with the boundary at the edge of the first horizon bucket rather than at a tuned
value.

```{python}
#| label: fig-sweep
#| fig-cap: "WQL against context length, per horizon segment, relative to reading the whole history. Below zero: better than zero-shot. 46 lengths, log scale."
#| code-fold: true
lengths = sorted(
    int(path.name.split("_")[1])
    for path in EXPERIMENTS.glob(f"context_*_{ROSTER}.parquet")
    if path.name.split("_")[1].isdigit() and "origin" not in path.name
)
segment = (pl.when(pl.col("horizon") <= 7).then(pl.lit("days 01-07"))
             .otherwise(pl.lit("days 08-28")).alias("segment"))
sweep = pl.concat([
    metrics.score(on_validation(experiment(f"context_{length}")).with_columns(segment),
                  by="segment").with_columns(pl.lit(length).alias("context_length"))
    for length in lengths
])

longest = max(lengths)
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(11, 3.5))
for name, colour in [("days 01-07", plots.MAIN_COLOR), ("days 08-28", plots.TEXT_COLOR)]:
    part = sweep.filter(pl.col("segment") == name).sort("context_length")
    reference = part.filter(pl.col("context_length") == longest)["WQL"][0]
    ax.plot(part["context_length"].to_numpy(),
            100 * (part["WQL"].to_numpy() / reference - 1),
            color=colour, marker="o", markersize=4, linewidth=1.5, label=name)
ax.axhline(0, color=plots.MUTED_TEXT_COLOR, linewidth=1)
ax.set_xscale("log")
ax.set_xlabel("context length (days, log scale)")
ax.set_ylabel("WQL vs whole history (%)")
ax.legend()
plt.show()
```

```{python}
#| echo: false
plateaus = {}
for name in ("days 01-07", "days 08-28"):
    part = sweep.filter(pl.col("segment") == name)
    best = part.sort("WQL").row(0, named=True)
    within = part.filter(pl.col("WQL") <= best["WQL"] * 1.01)["context_length"].sort()
    plateaus[name] = {"best": best["context_length"], "within": len(within),
                      "shortest": int(within.min())}
```

The shape of that curve is the finding. Below roughly 150 days the model is badly starved and
the error rises steeply; above a few hundred it is **flat to within a fraction of a
percent**. The best length is `{python} f"{plateaus['days 01-07']['best']:,}"` days for the
first week and `{python} f"{plateaus['days 08-28']['best']:,}"` for days 8–28 — but neither
is a point, it is a plateau: `{python} f"{plateaus['days 01-07']['within']}"` and
`{python} f"{plateaus['days 08-28']['within']}"` lengths score within 1% of those winners.

Two more things say the same. The same selection rule run on WMAPE instead of WQL picks 1,288
for the early segment rather than 1,099 — the same flat region, a different point in it. And
a CPU run and a GPU run of the sweep disagreed on the coarse-grid winner, yet still selected
the same final pair.

```{python}
#| label: tbl-stitch
#| tbl-cap: "The stitch against reading the whole history, validation window."
#| code-fold: true
choice = read_json("context_choice")
early, late = choice["early_context"], choice["late_context"]
from m5_chronos2.chronos2 import stitch

candidates = {
    "whole history": experiment(f"context_{longest}"),
    f"best single length ({late})": experiment(f"context_{late}"),
    f"stitch {early} -> {late}": stitch(experiment(f"context_{early}"),
                                        experiment(f"context_{late}"),
                                        choice["early_last_day"]),
}
md(metrics.compare({k: on_validation(v) for k, v in candidates.items()},
                   reference="whole history")
   .select(["model"] + [f"{m}_change_%" for m in metrics.METRICS]), digits=3)
```

So: a 46-length sweep, a selection rule fixed in advance, a stitch across two regimes — and
the whole exercise removes **0.11% of WMAPE and 0.08% of WQL.** Reading the entire history,
which is what the model does if you tell it nothing, is within a tenth of a percent of the
best configuration available. Remember, though, that in this setting no series was longer
than 2,000 days, which is far from the maximum capacity of Chronos-2: on a different model,
or with more history, the relationship between performance and context length could be
**non-monotonic**.

## LoRA for finetuning

Full fine-tuning would update 120M weights. LoRA (for low rank) freezes them and learns a
low-rank correction to the attention matrices and the output layer — a few hundred thousand
trainable parameters, which is what makes the run affordable at all. I used rank 8, learning
rate 1e-5, 1,000 steps, effective batch 32.

The protocol keeps the fine-tune away from what it is scored on:

```{python}
#| label: fig-lora-protocol
#| fig-cap: "How one fine-tune is laid out around its forecast origin. The history band is drawn truncated; in practice it runs back to the start of the series. The best checkpoint by the validation loss is the one kept."
#| code-fold: true
SPAN = 200                                  # days of history drawn, for scale only
window_starts = np.random.default_rng(0).integers(-SPAN, -56, size=6)

                           (-28, 0, plots.MUTED_TEXT_COLOR),
                           (0, 28, plots.MAIN_COLOR)]:

for row, start in enumerate(window_starts):
```

The test-origin model is a fresh fine-tune with the same configuration on the longer history,
exactly as the baseline was retrained per window.

Nothing forces a model to forecast at the context length it was tuned at, so I tried all four
pairings.

```{python}
#| label: tbl-lora
#| tbl-cap: "Validation WQL per segment. Lower is better; the two winners are the configuration carried forward."
#| code-fold: true
def lora(finetune_context, predict_context, origin):
    name = (f"lora_ctx{finetune_context}_steps1000_lr1e-05_bs32"
            f"_context_{predict_context}_origin{origin}")
    return experiment(name)

configs = {f"zero-shot, context {c}": experiment(f"context_{c}") for c in (early, late)}
for finetune_context in (late, early):
    for predict_context in (early, late):
        configs[f"LoRA@{finetune_context}, context {predict_context}"] = lora(
            finetune_context, predict_context, VALIDATION_ORIGIN)

by_segment = metrics.compare(
    {name: on_validation(table).with_columns(segment) for name, table in configs.items()},
    by="segment")
md(by_segment.select("model", "segment", "WQL").pivot(on="segment", index="model",
                                                      values="WQL"), digits=6)
```

Per-segment winners: **fine-tuned at 1,099 and forecasting at 1,099 for days 1–7, fine-tuned
at 1,099 and forecasting at 1,829 for days 8–28.** The long segment is a *cross* pairing —
tuned at one length, predicting at another — which training and predicting at a single length
would never have surfaced. It is also the kind of result that is easy to over-read: the gap
between it and the next configuration is in the fourth decimal of WQL.

The cost is worth recording. On my laptop CPU this measured 21.7 s per training step and
126 s per evaluation pass: 6.4 hours per fine-tune, up to 25.5 hours for the whole plan. The
same work on a GTX 1080 took about 32 minutes per run. Every number in this section that I
compare with another was produced on the same device, because CPU and GPU disagree in the
third decimal of WQL — enough to invert a ranking this tight.

## What this adds up to

Out of the box, on data it had never seen, Chronos-2 beat a reproduced top-4% feature
pipeline by 8.3% of WMAPE. Then: four families of extra information, a 46-length sweep of its
most important setting, a stitch across horizon segments and a LoRA fine-tune — and the
combined effect of all of it is **a fraction of a percent, in the wrong direction on the
point forecast.** These results really highlight the power of the model's architecture — it
isn't even a heavy-duty product with its 120M parameters, compared to GPT-3's **175B
parameters**: and that was in 2020!

question is not "which knob did I miss" but **"is this a limit of the tuning, or of the
problem?"** — and that one is answerable, which is what [the third post](../03-ceiling/) does.
It also weighs the one lever I never pulled: **treat different series differently** —
fine-tune per group, cross-learn inside a group, one model per group. The possibilities there
are endless, which is exactly why the question deserves an answer before any of them is built.
